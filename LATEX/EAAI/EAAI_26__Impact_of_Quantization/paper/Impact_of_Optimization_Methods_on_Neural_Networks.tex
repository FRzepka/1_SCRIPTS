\section{Introduction}
With the increasing demand for clean energy, driven by environmental concerns, energy storage systems play a crucial role in achieving this goal \cite{lin_towards_2022, ngoy_lithium-ion_2025}. Given the high power and energy density required for energy storage systems, Lithium-Ion batteries appear to be the perfect match \cite{chen_review_2021, khan_maximizing_2023}. Furthermore, Lithium-Ion batteries have a relatively high cycle life and a low self-discharge capability \cite{zhang_cycle_2024}. To ensure the safe and efficient operation of these systems, a battery management system (BMS) is used to measure and monitor voltage, current, and temperature, and to estimate \ac{soh} \cite{ali_towards_2019}. The \ac{soh} is defined as: 
\begin{equation}
    \text{SOH} = \frac{Q_\text{current}}{Q_\text{initial}} 
\end{equation}
Where $Q_\text{current}$ and $Q_\text{initial}$ are the current actual usable capacity and the initial capacity of the battery. The \ac{soh} is used as a metric for quantifying battery degradation \cite{tao_data-based_2024}, where $100 \%$ represents a new battery. As degradation progresses, the percentage decreases until a defined end-of-life \cite{jin_overview_2021}. Accurate \ac{soh} estimation remains a challenging task that has received significant attention in the research community \cite{yao_fast-adaptive_2025, tran_comprehensive_2021}, especially under the strict compute and memory constraints of embedded BMS hardware \cite{hu_onet_2026}. There are different \ac{soh} estimation approaches that vary in complexity. Physics-based methods require computationally intensive partial differential equations to simulate the dynamic behavior of a battery \cite{iurilli_physics-based_2022, tu_integrating_2023}. Equivalent circuit models (ECM) are simpler and lighter models but rely on extensive experimental testing and prior knowledge \cite{noura_review_2020, chang_improvement_2022}. 
In recent years, data-driven methods have received increasing attention in \ac{soh} Estimation research \cite{sylvestrin_state_2025}. These methods do not require knowledge of the battery's internal processes; instead, they aim to learn the aging mechanism from historical data \cite{yao_fast-adaptive_2025}. In particular, deep learning approaches encompass different types of architectures, such as \acp{lstm}, \acp{gru}, \acp{cnn}, and \acp{tcn} \cite{sylvestrin_state_2025, yang_state_2024}, which have been used for \ac{soh} estimation. However, deploying parameter-heavy deep learning architectures on resource-constrained microcontrollers requires optimization techniques such as pruning and quantization \cite{schulze_pq_2025}. To this end, Tiny Machine Learning (TinyML) enables edge execution \cite{pau_tiny_2024}, but it remains unclear how different deep learning architectures respond to compression techniques in terms of accuracy and inference time. 
This study addresses this gap by investigating whether the effect of compression on accuracy and inference time is architecture-dependent. Therefore, we train and compress these four architectures for \ac{soh} estimation and deploy them on an STM32H753ZI-class microcontroller subject to model size, latency and energy consumption (section 3). Then we analyze how each architecture's accuracy and inference time degrade under pruning and quantization (section 4). The models are trained with the Lithium Iron Phosphate (LFP) DoE Cycle Aging Dataset \cite{rzepka_design_2025}, in which the batteries were long-term cycled under laboratory conditions, including constant temperatures, regular check-ups, and hybrid pulse power characterization (HPPC) tests. Finally, we discuss the implications of these results in Section 5. We make our analysis publicly available \footnote{GitHub repo: \url{https://github.com/hpides/batmm}}.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*] 
\item This study investigates whether compression effects are architecture-dependent and whether different model families remain equally usable after optimization.
\item The paper therefore compares recurrent and convolutional architectures using a single, consistent pipeline from training to embedded benchmarking.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Related Work and Research Gap}{Related Work and Research Gap}}
Deep Learning approaches for estimating \ac{soh} can be divided into recurrent and convolutional networks. For recurrent models, \acp{lstm} are often used because they can capture long-term temporal aging patterns. For example, Nguyen Van et al. \cite{nguyen_van_estimation_2023} developed a two-stage \ac{lstm} for \ac{soh} and internal resistance estimation, outperforming a reference Feedforward Neural Network (FNN) in accuracy. To improve accuracy and robustness, Xu et al. \cite{xu_lstm-based_2024} incorporated data characteristics and spatio-temporal attention into an \ac{lstm}-based \ac{soh}-estimation method. Ungurean et al. \cite{ungurean_online_2020} trained a \ac{gru} using the adaptive moment estimation algorithm, resulting in a significantly smaller parameter footprint than an \ac{lstm} with only a slightly higher estimation error, making it a suitable candidate for an embedded implementation on a BMS. Similarly, Vaishnani et al. \cite{vaishnani_deep_2024} observed that a \ac{gru} achieved better performance ($\text{\ac{rmse}} = 0.017$) over \ac{lstm}, RNN, and hybrid \ac{lstm}-\ac{gru} models.

In contrast, convolutional architectures like the \ac{tcn} enable parallel processing. Yang et al. \cite{yang_state_2024} proposed a \ac{tcn} with an attention mechanism, which achieved an average \ac{rmse} of $2.10\%$, outperforming a \ac{cnn}, support vector regression, and a basic \ac{tcn}. Furthermore, Bi et al. \cite{bi_performance_2022} showed that the \ac{tcn} outperformed the \ac{lstm} by $3\%$ in accuracy under accelerated aging conditions. Additionally, Aliberti et al. \cite{aliberti_comparative_2022} evaluated an \ac{lstm}, a \ac{gru}, a one-dimensional \ac{cnn}, and a hybrid \ac{cnn}-\ac{lstm}; in that study, the one-dimensional \ac{cnn} yielded the lowest estimation error and prediction variance.

While the studies above focus on estimation accuracy, fewer works address deployment under resource constraints. Kim et al. \cite{kim_edge-compatible_2025} propose an approach to developing a lightweight, edge-compatible deep learning framework for \ac{soh} estimation. They reduced the model size by $99\%$ using knowledge distillation, structured pruning, and dynamic quantization and outperformed a MobileNetV1-based lightweight baseline in both accuracy and compactness. 

Existing studies either compare multiple neural network architectures for \ac{soh} estimation without considering compression techniques, or apply compression techniques to a single architecture \cite{kim_edge-compatible_2025}. Consequently, it remains unexplored whether different architectures respond differently to compression techniques such as pruning and quantization in terms of accuracy and inference time under resource-constrained conditions.  

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]

\item Existing \ac{soh} studies report promising sequence-model performance, but many evaluations remain model-specific and not directly comparable across architectures.
\item Compression literature often focuses on algorithmic gains in isolation and rarely combines battery-domain performance with deployment-level KPIs.
\item The open gap is a controlled cross-architecture analysis that links compression decisions directly to BMS-relevant engineering costs.
\item Figure 2 (planned, position in this section): literature positioning map contrasting accuracy-centric studies, compression-centric studies, and the proposed integrated approach.
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Comparison Objective and Benchmark Design}
The comparison is designed to determine whether the effects of model optimization transfer consistently across neural architectures or depend on the mechanism used to represent temporal battery behavior. For this purpose, strong uncompressed baseline models are first established for the \ac{lstm}, \ac{gru}, \ac{cnn}, and \ac{tcn} families. Structured pruning and quantization are then applied under controlled conditions. The benchmark therefore examines not only whether these methods reduce model size and embedded execution cost, but also whether the resulting loss of \ac{soh} prediction accuracy follows a comparable pattern across architectures. This makes it possible to identify cases in which an expected compression benefit generalizes across model families and cases in which a particular architecture is more sensitive to parameter removal or reduced numerical precision.

The comparison is organized around the change from each architecture-specific FP32 baseline rather than around baseline accuracy alone. Prediction quality is evaluated using \ac{mae} and \ac{rmse}, while deployment cost is characterized through model footprint and the embedded metrics model size, latency, and energy. Model sizes may differ where this is required to establish competitive baseline quality. However, the data partition, target definition, preprocessing, error metrics, optimization settings, and benchmark protocol are held fixed wherever applicable. The resulting accuracy--resource trade-offs provide the basis for selecting optimization strategies according to both architecture and deployment constraints.

\subsection{Data, Target, and Split Strategy}
The experiments use the publicly available LFP DoE Cycle Aging Dataset recorded with 15 commercial 1.8-Ah, 3.2-V LFP 18650 cells at a controlled ambient temperature of $25\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ \cite{ProductSpecificationJGCFR186501800mAh32V,rzepka_design_2025}. The mixed full- and fractional-factorial design varies charge rate, discharge rate, and depth of discharge. Its factorial core covers $C_{\mathrm{chg}}=0.5$--$0.9\,\mathrm{C}$, $C_{\mathrm{dis}}=1.0$--$2.5\,\mathrm{C}$, and a depth of discharge of $45$--$65\,\%$, with additional boundary conditions extending the investigated operating range. Periodic check-ups comprising capacity tests, open-circuit-voltage sweeps, and hybrid pulse-power characterization track degradation between the aging loops. Stationary-storage load profiles complement the controlled cycling conditions with application-oriented power trajectories \cite{kucevicStandardBatteryEnergy2020}. The raw 1-Hz records contain voltage, current, terminal temperature, and auxiliary measurements.

The prediction target is the capacity-based state of health, defined as the ratio between the measured available capacity and the nominal capacity. Capacity references are obtained from the periodic check-ups and interpolated along each aging trajectory. Figure~\ref{fig:lfp_soh_trajectories} shows the resulting reference trajectories and their pronounced spread across operating conditions. Local increases between successive check-ups reflect variability in the measured available capacity and do not indicate a reversal of long-term aging. For model training, one \ac{soh} target is assigned to each hourly feature vector.

The partition is performed by complete cells to prevent samples from one cell from appearing in more than one subset. Cells C01, C03, C05, C07, C09, C13, C17, C19, C25, and C27 form the training set, C15 and C21 form the validation set, and C11, C23, and C29 are retained exclusively for testing. This cell-level split and the resulting hourly samples are held fixed across all four architecture families.

\subsection{Architecture Portfolio and Baseline Selection}
The portfolio comprises two recurrent architectures, an \ac{lstm} and a \ac{gru}, and two convolutional architectures, a \ac{cnn} and a \ac{tcn}. This selection enables compression to be compared across different mechanisms for temporal representation instead of within a single model family. All four models receive the same 20-channel input representation obtained from the hourly mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum of voltage, current, temperature, EFC, and cumulative charge. One \ac{soh} target is assigned to each hourly interval. Each model is causal and produces one \ac{soh} estimate at every time step.

The recurrent models use a learned feature projection followed by a unidirectional recurrent stack, layer normalization, residual MLP blocks, and an MLP output head. The selected \ac{lstm} contains two recurrent layers with a hidden size of 192 and three residual blocks. The \ac{gru} uses three recurrent layers with a hidden size of 160 and four residual blocks. The convolutional models process the same sequences using residual causal convolutions. The \ac{cnn} contains three blocks with 128 channels, a kernel size of five, and dilation factors of 1, 2, and 4. The \ac{tcn} extends this dilation pattern to 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 across five blocks with 96 channels, thereby providing a wider temporal context without recurrent state propagation. Table~\ref{tab:arch_portfolio} summarizes the selected uncompressed FP32 baselines.

Baseline selection followed two stages. First, a family-specific hyperparameter search determined a candidate configuration using validation \ac{rmse}. Second, a local capacity sweep evaluated coordinated reductions and increases in the internal widths while retaining the remaining architecture and training settings. The investigated hidden or channel widths were 64--256 for the \ac{lstm}, 48--256 for the \ac{gru}, 64--224 for the \ac{cnn}, and 48--144 for the \ac{tcn}. The final reference configuration was selected from this sweep by validation performance, while the test cells were reserved for the subsequent comparison. This procedure checks the neighborhood of the selected capacity and avoids basing the compression study on an arbitrary model size.

\subsection{Training and Evaluation Protocol}
All architectures were trained with the same cell-level partitioning and random seed of 42. A robust scaler was fitted exclusively to the training cells and subsequently applied without refitting to the validation and test cells. The hourly feature vectors were arranged as overlapping causal sequences with a stride of one. Their architecture-specific lengths are listed in Table~\ref{tab:arch_portfolio}. Each model was trained in a sequence-to-sequence setting and therefore returned one \ac{soh} estimate for every hourly step. The first 8 steps were excluded from the loss for the recurrent models, compared with 12 steps for the \ac{cnn} and 16 steps for the \ac{tcn}, to reduce the influence of state initialization and incomplete convolutional context.

The training objective combined the mean squared prediction error with a temporal smoothness penalty,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}
    = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\hat{y}_i-y_i\right)^2
    + \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathcal{R}\!\left(\Delta\hat{y}\right),
    \qquad
    \Delta\hat{y}_t=\hat{y}_t-\hat{y}_{t-1},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}$ denotes the mean absolute step change for the \ac{lstm}, \ac{cnn}, and \ac{tcn} and the mean squared step change for the \ac{gru}. The smoothness term discourages implausible hour-to-hour fluctuations without imposing a fixed degradation trajectory. AdamW was used for parameter optimization, followed by a cosine-annealing schedule with warm restarts. Gradient clipping and early stopping limited unstable updates and unnecessary training. Table~\ref{tab:training_protocol} reports the architecture-specific settings retained after the hyperparameter search.

Validation was performed every three epochs. The checkpoint with the lowest validation \ac{rmse} was retained, with an early-stopping patience of 12 epochs for the \ac{lstm}, \ac{gru}, and \ac{tcn} and 10 epochs for the \ac{cnn}. The held-out test cells were used only after model and capacity selection. Prediction quality was quantified by
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\hat{y}_i-y_i\right|,
    \qquad
    \mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\hat{y}_i-y_i\right)^2}.
\end{equation}
During baseline evaluation, each of the three held-out test cells was processed as one continuous causal trajectory. The recurrent states of the \ac{lstm} and \ac{gru} were propagated throughout each trajectory, while the \ac{cnn} and \ac{tcn} retained the input history required by their causal receptive fields across successive processing blocks. The metrics were first calculated separately for each test cell and then macro-averaged so that every cell contributed equally to the reported baseline result. The subsequent compression and embedded benchmarks retain their architecture-specific execution modes. The \ac{cnn} and \ac{tcn} process non-overlapping model input windows, whereas the recurrent states of the \ac{lstm} and \ac{gru} are propagated sample by sample. All reported metrics use unsmoothed model outputs and the dimensionless 0--1 \ac{soh} scale. Accordingly, an \ac{mae} or \ac{rmse} of 0.01 corresponds to one \ac{soh} percentage point on the 0--100\% scale.

\subsection{Model Optimization Methods} \label{sub:model_optimization}


\mypar{Pruning}
Pruning, in the scope of our work, is the process of removing weights from a model. Typically, the model components that contribute the least to a model's performance are candidates for removal. Pruning can be classified as either \textit{unstructured} or \textit{structured}. In unstructured pruning, we pinpoint the exact, individual weights we seek to remove. As the name suggests, the resulting model loses its structure; thus, to keep the matrices in an integral form, the selected weights are simply set to zero. We achieve high sparsity with minimal loss in accuracy, but the irregular matrices do not run faster on standard hardware that does not support sparse execution. In structured pruning, entire weight clusters, such as matrix rows/columns, filters, and channels, are removed. This physically shrinks the model architecture, making it faster and more efficient on typical hardware, but usually comes with a sharper drop in accuracy than unstructured pruning.

We incorporate structured pruning by using the TorchPruner library~\cite{torchpruner}.

\mypar{Distillation}
Knowledge distillation transfers information from a larger, pretrained \textit{teacher} model to a smaller \textit{student} model. During training, the student learns not only from the ground-truth \ac{soh} values but also from the teacher's predictions, which provide additional information about the function learned by the larger model. This helps the student retain prediction accuracy despite its reduced capacity. Once training is complete, only the student is deployed, so distillation introduces no additional inference latency overhead. In our experiments, as the teacher for a compressed student of the same architecture, we evaluate both the uncompressed model and the previously pruned model in the iterative pruning setting.



\mypar{Quantization}
Quantization refers to reducing the precision of model parameters, usually the weights. Typical models are stored in FP32 (32-bit floating point), meaning each parameter uses 32 bits to represent it. When quantized, these parameters would then only be saved with e.g., 16 (FP16), 8 (INT8) or 4 (INT4) bits. This directly results in smaller file sizes. If the underlying hardware supports the quantized representation, these models can be executed significantly faster at minimal accuracy loss. Quantization can be paired with pruning to leverage both techniques. In this case, the model is usually pruned first, and the resulting, sparse model is quantized afterward.

For our experiments, we quantize to 8-bit precision, which compresses our models from FP32 to INT8.

\subsection{Embedded Benchmark Protocol}

For our benchmarks, we use a Python script to iterate over the folder of compressed models generated by the methods in Subsection \ref{sub:model_optimization}. We benchmark all model variants with the same target platform and firmware toolchain. The script creates an isolated project in STM32CubeIDE (version 2.1.1) using STM32CubeMX (version 6.17) and STEdgeAI (version 4.0) for the machine-learning backend, then flashes it to our H753ZI microcontroller. The microcontroller is also connected to a USB AVHzY CT-3 power meter, which measures power draw over 100-millisecond intervals. An overview of our benchmarking setup is shown in Figure \ref{fig:benchmark_setup}.

After building the project, we sequentially evaluate the test set of battery cells on the microcontroller using the input-window lengths specified in Table \ref{tab:arch_portfolio}. Inference is performed with a batch size of 1 because deployment is stateful and operates sample-by-sample in online BMS setups. For the \ac{cnn} and \ac{tcn}, the complete input windows are sufficiently small to be processed in a single inference pass. The recurrent networks, \ac{lstm} and \ac{gru}, exceeded the microcontroller's available runtime memory, primarily because of the memory required for intermediate activations. We execute one step at a time and explicitly preserve their recurrent states, i.e., hidden and cell states for \ac{lstm}, and only the hidden states for \ac{gru}. Provided that the states are initialized and propagated consistently, this step-wise execution is mathematically equivalent to processing the complete input sequence in a single inference call. To ensure fairness, we track results only after 192 and 168 steps, respectively.

\mypar{Metrics} We track the \ac{mae}, its bias, and the maximum absolute error between the compressed model's battery \ac{soh} prediction and the ground truth. We measure the average inference latency of a single prediction and the size of the compressed models on disk. With our power meter, we measure the average voltage, current, and power consumption during inference.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Baseline Model Comparison}
The uncompressed FP32 models were first compared independently of any optimization. Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_model_comparison}(a) reports cell-macro errors across the three held-out test cells using the 0--1 \ac{soh} scale defined in Section~3.4. The \ac{mae} is 0.0172 for the \ac{cnn}, 0.0146 for the \ac{gru}, 0.0167 for the \ac{lstm}, and 0.0152 for the \ac{tcn}. The corresponding \ac{rmse} values are 0.0224, 0.0180, 0.0199, and 0.0196. The \ac{gru} therefore provides the lowest cell-macro error for both metrics in this baseline comparison, while the \ac{tcn} follows closely with the smallest model footprint.

The parameter footprints differ more clearly than the prediction errors. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_model_comparison}(b), the \ac{tcn} and \ac{cnn} require 1.678 and 1.920 MiB of FP32 weights, respectively, compared with 3.221 MiB for the \ac{gru} and 3.418 MiB for the \ac{lstm}. The largest baseline is therefore approximately twice the size of the smallest one despite their similar prediction accuracy. These differences provide distinct starting points for evaluating the architecture-dependent effects of compression in the following sections.

\mypar{Accuracy}
Figure \ref{fig:errors} compares prediction errors across all four architectures, showing the mean, maximum, and bias of the errors. 

For the \ac{cnn}, we examine that the uncompressed base model does not fit in the limited memory of the microcontroller. While both quantized and non-quantized models slightly increase \ac{mae}, with up to 60\% of parameters remaining, they both improve as pruning continues, with the quantized model performing best on average at 30\% of parameters remaining; only the \ac{rmse} is marginally larger. Even with only 15\% remaining, the model performs as well as the completely unpruned model. 

The bias remains steady across all levels of pruning, indicating that the model over- and underpredicts equally.

\ac{tcn} exhibits irregular behavior, incorporating multiple severe spikes and slopes in its mean prediction error. The quantized model slightly outperforms the base model at moderate pruning levels, both on average and, especially, in terms of a smaller \ac{rmse}. \ac{tcn} is also the only model for which the bias is not symmetrical, as in early pruning stages, when the model collapses, it tends to underpredict. Experiments have shown that the model predicted a constant value, resulting in an inherent impact on the error.

Both recurrent architectures fit only in quantized form on the microcontroller. Since the \ac{lstm} and \ac{gru} blocks themselves cannot be structurally pruned, we prune only the neural network part of each, which accounts for roughly half of the parameter count.

For the \ac{lstm}, mean and squared error trends are relatively equal. Similar to the \ac{cnn}, the error is worse in the early pruning stages but peaks when a quarter of the parameters are removed. The model is overpredicting the error by a small margin.

\ac{gru} shows a similar trend; however, the maximum error steadily increases here. Compared to \ac{lstm}, pruning up to 60\% of the parameters still yields a well-performing model.

For all models, the INT8 version achieves the lowest error at relatively moderate pruning levels, while aggressive pruning falls within the same range as the base model, even for unquantized models.

\mypar{Inference Latency}
Figure \ref{fig:latency} shows the inference latency for predicting a single battery value across all four architectures.

For \acp{cnn}, we see a linear decline in latency for both unquantized and quantized models. The quantized model shows a constant speed difference of roughly 850 ms relative to the unquantized model, resulting in the fastest time for a single prediction at 320 ms.

\ac{tcn} is faster than the \ac{cnn} at every pruning degree with a similar symmetric trend. Even though it needs comparatively more input steps, the \ac{tcn} is smaller and has fewer parameters. But because the \ac{tcn} has fewer prunable parameters than the \ac{tcn}, it caps out earlier and has a minimum latency of only 340 ms. The unquantized \ac{tcn} model is closer in latency to its quantized variant than the \ac{cnn} model.

\ac{lstm} and \ac{gru} both exhibit near-constant speeds of 280 ms and 195 ms, respectively. Pruning the surrounding multi-layer perceptron yields a negligible latency reduction of less than 5 ms. This shows that the recurrent layers we do not prune are the dominant factor in inference speed.

While pruning recurrent architectures does not yield latency improvements, they are faster than convolutional architectures at every degree of pruning.

\mypar{Model Size}
Figure \ref{fig:size} shows the effect of pruning on the model size on disk. 

The \ac{cnn} is too large to fit on the microcontroller in its uncompressed form. While the unquantized architecture is always more memory-intensive than its quantized counterpart, pruning is, as expected, more effective because it removes 32 bits per parameter rather than 8. The same bit-width difference explains the consistent fourfold gap between the two model sizes across all pruning degrees.

The same constant decrease in size can be examined for the \ac{tcn} and the recurrent architectures within their respective pruning capabilities. While the non-recurrent parts of the \ac{lstm} and \ac{gru} networks do not affect latency, they still account for roughly half of the architecture's size.

Overall, both compression techniques consistently reduce size across all model types.

\mypar{Electrical Power}
Figure \ref{fig:electrical_power} reports the average USB-input voltage, current, power, and energy cost per prediction of the complete microcontroller board during inference. 

For all architectures, the supply voltage remains nearly constant at 5.08–5.10 V across compression levels, with quantized models being marginally lower than unquantized ones. Consequently, the observed power variations, according to \(P=U \cdot I\), are primarily caused by changes in current draw. 

For the CNN, pruning generally reduces the average current and power, most clearly for the INT8 variants, whose power decreases from approximately 1.50 W to 1.34 W. The TCN shows a similar but less regular tendency, including an isolated drop for the FP32 model at approximately 40\% of parameters remaining.

In contrast, the quantized LSTM and GRU remain nearly constant at 1.28–1.31 W. This behavior is consistent with the latency results because only the surrounding feed-forward layers are pruned, while the recurrent layers, which dominate execution time, remain unchanged. 

The INT8 convolutional models occasionally exhibit higher average power than their FP32 counterparts; however, this does not necessarily imply greater energy consumption, as the quantized models execute considerably faster, as seen in the energy-related plots. Consequently, the energy-per-prediction curves largely follow the latency trends shown in Figure \ref{fig:latency}, because variations in inference time dominate the comparatively small variations in average power. Thus, the reduced latency of an INT8 model can offset its higher instantaneous power draw, since energy per prediction equals the product of average power and inference time.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Baseline Architecture Comparison (Flo, EET)}
The baseline comparison shows that architecture choice affects resource demand more clearly than \ac{soh} prediction accuracy. Although all four models operate within a narrow \ac{mae} and \ac{rmse} range, their FP32 parameter footprints differ by approximately a factor of two. The \ac{gru} achieves the lowest cell-macro \ac{mae} and \ac{rmse}, whereas the \ac{tcn} provides the smallest baseline footprint. Consequently, no architecture is uniformly superior across prediction error and storage requirements. The appropriate baseline depends on whether the application prioritizes estimation accuracy or memory demand.

The local capacity sweeps further show that increasing the number of parameters does not produce a monotonic accuracy improvement. Compact configurations can remain competitive, while larger variants may generalize less effectively despite their additional capacity. This finding is important for the subsequent optimization comparison because it separates compression effects from arbitrary over-sizing of the original network. Each architecture must first be evaluated near a suitable capacity before the effect of pruning or quantization can be interpreted fairly. The trajectory comparison supports this conclusion at the single-cell level: all selected models reproduce the long-term degradation trend, but their local deviations occur at different points in time. Quantitative metrics over the complete benchmark trajectory are therefore required in addition to the visual comparison.

\section{Conclusion and Outlook}

\subsection{Summary of Optimization Results (HPI)}

This study presents a controlled comparison of compression techniques across \ac{cnn}, \ac{tcn}, \ac{lstm}, and \ac{gru} architectures for \ac{soh} prediction. The experiments show that the effectiveness of model compression is strongly architecture-dependent. Structured pruning reduces the latency of the convolutional architectures because it directly removes computational
operations. In contrast, pruning has almost no effect on the latency of the
LSTM and GRU models, since their recurrent layers remained unchanged and
continue to dominate execution time. INT8 quantization consistently reduces
model storage and is necessary for deploying the recurrent architectures on
the target microcontroller.

No single architecture is optimal for every deployment objective. The
compressed \ac{cnn} models provide the most favorable trade-off between model size
and prediction accuracy, whereas the \ac{gru} achieves the lowest inference
latency. Among the uncompressed baselines, the \ac{gru} provides the lowest cell-macro \ac{mae} and \ac{rmse}, while the \ac{tcn} has the smallest FP32 parameter footprint. Power consumption largely
follows the latency trends, indicating that shorter execution time is more
important than small variations in power.

Overall, the central contribution of this work is a reproducible, accuracy-cost analysis that connects model optimization directly to embedded design decisions. Rather than recommending one universally optimal model, the results provide a basis for selecting an architecture and
compression level according to the application's accuracy, memory, latency,
and power constraints.

\subsection{Implications for Battery Management Systems (Flo, EET and Pavel, EET)}
For a BMS, optimization is useful only when the reduction in resource demand preserves an \ac{soh} estimate that remains sufficiently accurate for monitoring, maintenance planning, and operating-limit adaptation. The combined structured-pruning and INT8-quantization variants reduce parameter storage by approximately 80.0--81.5\% for the \ac{cnn}, \ac{gru}, and \ac{lstm}, while the absolute change in normalized \ac{mae} remains below 0.0008. These results indicate that substantial storage reductions are possible without a corresponding increase of the same magnitude in estimation error. The small accuracy improvements observed for some variants should not be interpreted as a general benefit of compression, but rather as evidence that the optimized models remain within the accuracy range of their baselines.

The \ac{tcn} result also demonstrates why a BMS implementation cannot be selected from prediction accuracy or a nominal precision label alone. Although the \ac{tcn} is the smallest FP32 baseline, its current combined optimization path reduces parameter storage by only 29.2\%. In particular, quantization provides no further storage reduction after pruning for the evaluated implementation. The achievable benefit, therefore, depends on the architecture, the supported operators, and the representation generated by the deployment toolchain. Relative compression and absolute deployed footprint must consequently be considered together.

From a system perspective, reduced model storage can create flash headroom for firmware, diagnostics, communication functions, and future model updates. However, parameter count and stored model size do not directly determine runtime RAM, latency, or energy consumption. These quantities must be evaluated on the target hardware because conversion overhead and operator support can offset theoretical savings.
