\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}
This dissertation investigates deployment-oriented artificial intelligence for battery management systems and combines model development, robustness analysis, embedded implementation, and hardware integration within one common research framework.

\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
This dissertation develops a deployment-oriented perspective on artificial intelligence for battery management systems. The work begins with neural-network-based ageing estimation for stationary storage systems, continues with robustness benchmarking of embedded battery state estimation under disturbed measurements, and then addresses the transfer of SOC and SOH models to microcontrollers using pruning and quantization. A comparative analysis across model architectures extends the compression perspective, while a dedicated hardware chapter documents the custom battery management system platform and the practical route from algorithm development to embedded execution. The central result is that battery intelligence must be evaluated not only by nominal prediction accuracy, but against a combined set of battery-management requirements across performance, hardware, and deployment.

\chapter*{Executive Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Executive Summary}
This dissertation is structured as a continuous, application-oriented account of artificial intelligence in battery management. Its main focus lies on neural architectures for ageing estimation in stationary storage systems, the robustness of embedded state estimation under realistic measurement disturbances, the efficient execution of such models on microcontrollers through pruning and quantization, and their integration into a real hardware and BMS platform. The overarching idea is that AI-enabled BMS methods should be evaluated not only by accuracy, but along the three requirement dimensions of performance, hardware, and deployment.

\chapter{Introduction and Motivation}
\chapterrole{}

Battery management systems require reliable internal-state information to operate lithium-ion batteries safely, efficiently, and over long service intervals. Estimates of state of charge and state of health influence charging control, power limitation, lifetime-aware operation, and higher-level energy-management decisions. At the same time, battery behaviour becomes increasingly nonlinear once ageing, operating-point variation, and real measurement imperfections are taken into account. This creates a strong motivation for AI-based estimation methods that can capture complex relations beyond compact analytical descriptions.

Practical BMS deployment, however, imposes requirements that go beyond nominal prediction accuracy under controlled evaluation settings. Estimators must remain useful under disturbed measurements, limited computation and memory, causal execution, and real firmware and sensing constraints. These requirements become especially relevant in decentralized stationary-storage systems and remote microgrid-like applications, where battery supervision must remain locally available over long maintenance intervals and without permanent cloud or internet connectivity. The central scientific gap addressed in this dissertation is therefore not the absence of accurate battery-AI models as such, but the limited availability of a consistent evaluation and development framework that brings model quality, robustness, embedded feasibility, and hardware-level realization together.

\section{Research Objective and Requirement Dimensions}
The research objective of the dissertation is to develop and validate deployment-oriented neural-network methods for battery management systems along three linked requirement dimensions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The first requirement dimension addresses estimator performance. This includes not only nominal accuracy under controlled conditions, but also robustness against disturbed measurements, recovery behaviour after state inconsistency, and stable operation under realistic signal disturbances and imperfect online conditions.
\item The second dimension concerns hardware requirements. Battery-AI methods intended for practical BMS use must be judged against the limits of the target platform, including memory demand, runtime latency, integration constraints, and restrictions imposed by the measurement chain itself.
\item The third dimension focuses on deployment requirements from model concept to embedded realization. This includes feature engineering, architecture selection, compression, model-to-device transfer, adaptability, scalability, and the firmware-level steps required to turn a trained estimator into a deployable BMS component.
\end{itemize}
These three dimensions are treated as interdependent rather than separate evaluation layers. The central argument of the dissertation is that battery-AI methods become practically meaningful only when predictive quality, embedded realizability, and hardware-level integration are developed and assessed together.

\section{Scientific Contributions}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item At the conceptual level, the dissertation establishes a unified deployment-oriented framework for evaluating battery-AI methods across performance, hardware, and deployment requirements. This framework provides the common perspective through which the subsequent studies are interpreted rather than treating accuracy, robustness, and embedded realization as isolated concerns.
\item For ageing estimation in stationary-storage cells, the work introduces cumulative-current features and temporal lag construction as a compact way of representing ageing-relevant information. In the first study, this representation is examined with an MLP-based baseline; within the broader dissertation context, however, it serves mainly as the starting point of a larger development path that later extends toward recurrent architectures and deployment-oriented estimator design.
\item A further methodological contribution is the robustness benchmark for embedded battery state estimation. Here, direct-measurement, hybrid, and data-driven estimator classes are compared under controlled disturbances such as bias, noise, missing samples, timing irregularities, and initialization mismatch, so that estimator quality is assessed under deployment-relevant rather than purely nominal conditions.
\item On the implementation side, the dissertation analyses how SOC and SOH estimators can be transferred to microcontrollers through pruning, quantization, and deployment-oriented STM32 benchmarking. This part of the work links model design to concrete trade-offs in memory demand, runtime latency, and practical firmware integration.
\item The experimental basis of the dissertation is provided by the Battery Management System State-Estimation Test Platform (BMS-STEP), a custom hardware environment for repeatable embedded-AI studies within one common BMS architecture. This platform creates the technical basis for comparing interchangeable estimation methods under controlled and reproducible hardware conditions.
\item Taken together, these elements form a cumulative design perspective for deployment-oriented battery intelligence that connects representation, robustness, embedded optimization, and hardware integration. The overall contribution therefore lies not only in the individual studies, but also in their integration into one coherent development and evaluation logic.
\end{itemize}

\section{Specific Research Objectives}
From this overall framing, the dissertation pursues five specific research objectives:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item To identify how neural-network methods can represent battery ageing with sufficient practical utility for stationary-storage applications and long-term condition monitoring.
\item To assess under which disturbance conditions embedded battery state estimators preserve acceptable accuracy, convergence behaviour, and recovery capability.
\item To evaluate how AI-based SOC and SOH estimators can be transferred to microcontrollers through resource-aware pruning, quantization, and model-to-device adaptation.
\item To analyse under which architectural conditions pruning and quantization remain effective without disproportionately degrading predictive performance.
\item To define how a hardware platform and validation chain should be structured in order to support repeatable, hardware-level evaluation of AI-based BMS methods under realistic embedded conditions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Structure of the Dissertation and Development Path}
The dissertation follows a cumulative structure in which study-specific methods, results, and local interpretation remain within the contribution chapters, while the common technical basis and the cross-study implications are developed in the surrounding chapters.

Several contribution chapters build on previously published or manuscript-based work. In particular, the ageing-estimation study in Chapter~5 is adapted from the published neural-network architecture paper on SOH estimation for stationary storage systems.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a}

The progression of the work is intentionally staged. The ageing-estimation chapter first addresses how battery-relevant information can be represented in a neural estimator. The robustness benchmark then shifts the focus from nominal performance to disturbance-aware evaluation under realistic measurement disturbances. The embedded-AI chapter extends this perspective toward microcontroller deployment, where compression, memory, and latency become decisive design variables. The subsequent compression chapter generalizes this question across architectures, while the hardware chapter and the BMS-STEP platform provide the common laboratory basis on which these deployment questions can be tested under realistic embedded conditions.

The shared laboratory hardware basis of the dissertation is the Battery Management System State-Estimation Test Platform (BMS-STEP), a custom 4s research BMS developed to compare and exchange embedded state-estimation methods on one reproducible system basis. The final synthesis chapter integrates the findings across all studies and derives the cumulative contribution of the work to deployment-oriented battery intelligence.

\chapter{Battery-Management Requirement Framework}

Battery-management systems require estimation methods that satisfy several conditions simultaneously. In practical operation, a model must provide accurate and stable state information, remain robust under realistic measurement imperfections, fit within the available embedded resources, and be transferable into an executable BMS implementation. For this reason, the present work organizes the relevant battery-management requirements into three connected areas: performance requirements, hardware requirements, and deployment requirements. This framework provides the common basis on which the individual studies of the dissertation are interpreted. Each study emphasizes different requirement groups, but all of them contribute to the same overarching question of how neural-network methods can be developed for deployment-oriented battery management.

\section{Performance Requirements}
Performance requirements describe the estimation quality that a BMS must obtain from an AI-based method during operation. In this dissertation, the central criteria are accuracy, robustness, and convergence behaviour. Accuracy is required because the estimator must provide reliable state information under nominal conditions. Robustness becomes essential once the input signals are affected by bias, noise, missing samples, or timing irregularities. Convergence behaviour determines whether the estimator can recover in a controlled manner after initialization mismatch, signal disturbances, or temporary state inconsistency. These criteria are examined most directly in the robustness benchmark, but they also remain relevant when the results of the ageing-estimation and embedded-AI studies are interpreted in a broader deployment context.

\section{Hardware Requirements}
Hardware requirements arise because BMS estimators are executed on resource-limited embedded platforms rather than in unrestricted desktop environments. The most important quantities considered in this work are RAM usage, Flash usage, and inference time. These metrics determine whether a model can be executed on the target controller with sufficient timing margin and without exhausting the available memory. In addition, hardware-related conditions such as measurement quality, controller structure, and system integration influence which estimator concepts remain feasible in practice. These questions become particularly visible in the embedded-AI chapter, where memory footprint and latency are quantified directly, and in the hardware chapter, which provides the BMS-oriented environment in which such constraints can be assessed experimentally.

\section{Deployment Requirements}
Deployment requirements describe how readily a model can be transferred from research development into practical BMS use. In this dissertation, the most relevant aspects are model complexity, adaptability, and scalability. Model complexity influences both the effort of implementation and the feasibility of later compression and hardware transfer. Adaptability refers to the extent to which modelling concepts, feature constructions, and estimator structures can be reused or modified for related battery-state estimation tasks. Scalability concerns whether a method and its optimization strategy remain viable across different model classes and target platforms. This requirement area therefore connects feature design, architecture choice, pruning, quantization, and firmware transfer within one common perspective.

\section{Coverage of the Framework in the Dissertation}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The ageing-estimation study addresses primarily the performance and deployment sides of the framework. Its focus lies on how ageing-relevant battery information can be represented in a neural estimator and how this representation can be translated into useful prediction quality for stationary-storage data.
\item The robustness benchmark concentrates most strongly on performance requirements. It shows that estimator quality in battery management cannot be reduced to nominal accuracy alone, but must also include robustness against disturbed measurements and convergence behaviour after inconsistencies.
\item The embedded-AI study connects deployment requirements with hardware requirements. In this part of the dissertation, model transfer, pruning, quantization, RAM usage, Flash usage, and inference latency become central because they determine whether a method can actually be executed on a microcontroller target.
\item The hardware chapter provides the experimental and system-level basis for this framework. It establishes a BMS-oriented platform on which different estimation methods can be integrated, exchanged, and tested under reproducible embedded conditions.
\item The architecture-dependent compression study extends the deployment perspective toward more general design rules. It examines how strongly pruning and quantization depend on model class and how scalable such optimization strategies remain across different architectures.
\end{itemize}

Taken together, these studies cover complementary parts of one common battery-management requirement framework. The framework therefore functions as the conceptual link between the chapters and as the basis for the final synthesis of the dissertation.

\chapter{State of the Art and Technical Background}

\section{Battery Management Systems}
Battery management systems supervise cell voltages, current, temperature, balancing, protection paths, and communication with higher-level control systems. Within this functional structure, SOC and SOH are not isolated diagnostic variables, but central quantities for power limitation, charging control, lifetime-aware operation, and system-level energy management.

\section{Neural Networks for Battery Ageing and State Estimation}
Neural methods are attractive in battery applications because they can represent nonlinear signal relations, exploit temporal context, and combine measured and derived variables in a data-driven manner. At the same time, their practical value depends not only on predictive accuracy, but also on interpretability, training stability, robustness, and deployment effort.

\section{Embedded AI in Battery Applications}
Embedded AI in battery applications shifts the focus from model quality alone toward deployment quality. Relevant criteria therefore include not only prediction error, but also Flash demand, RAM demand, latency, numerical stability, reproducibility, and the effort required to transfer a model from high-level research code to microcontroller inference.

\section{Compression and Model-Hardware Co-Design}
Compression and model-hardware co-design become essential as soon as AI is treated as a practical BMS component. Pruning and quantization are therefore not secondary optimizations, but central design choices that influence memory footprint, runtime behaviour, numerical precision, and ultimately the feasibility of embedded deployment.

\chapter{Experimental Basis and Hardware Ecosystem}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The experimental basis of this work combines laboratory battery datasets, embedded execution targets, and the custom BMS-STEP hardware platform for battery management.
\item This combined setup makes it possible to study battery-related artificial intelligence not only as an offline modelling problem, but also as an embedded systems problem shaped by sensing, communication, firmware partitioning, and hardware constraints.
\item The resulting experimental ecosystem forms the common basis for the ageing-estimation work, the robustness benchmark, and the embedded implementation studies.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{NMC Dataset -- Stationary-Storage Ageing Estimation (Chapter~5)}

The first dataset consists of cyclic ageing tests on Li-ion INR18650 NMC cells carried out at Technische Universit\"at Berlin. The cells have a nominal capacity of \SI{2.6}{\ampere\hour}, a nominal voltage of \SI{3.7}{\volt}, a maximum charging voltage of \SI{4.2}{\volt}, and a discharge cut-off at \SI{2.75}{\volt}. In total, 14 cells are investigated across seven ageing scenarios, with two cells per scenario. The scenarios systematically vary four operating parameters: mean state of charge, depth of discharge, discharge C-rate, and operating temperature. This parametric design is intended to reproduce representative operating windows of stationary-storage systems rather than highly dynamic mobile profiles. Charging follows a CC-CV procedure; discharging uses constant current. Periodic capacity tests inserted at defined intervals track the SOH progression over time and supply the degradation labels used for model training.

The state of health is defined as the ratio of the current cell capacity to the nominal capacity,\autocite{baraiComparisonMethodologiesNoninvasive2019,kurzweilElektrochemischeSpeicherSuperkondensatoren2018}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SOH}(t) = \frac{C(t)}{C_\mathrm{N}},
\label{eq:soh_definition}
\end{equation}
where $C(t)$ is determined from periodic capacity tests in which the cell is fully charged and then discharged under controlled conditions. Because capacity tests exist only at discrete time points, the SOH label is linearly interpolated onto the continuous measurement timeline. Figure~\ref{fig:paper1_soh_progression} shows the resulting SOH progression for all 14 cells over both equivalent full cycles and absolute time in days. Temperature has a pronounced impact on degradation, whereas the interactions between the remaining parameters are less obvious from direct visual inspection alone.

Across both Chapters~6 and~7, the SOC and SOH labels are constructed from dataset-side processing. The SOC label is derived from Coulomb counting with drift compensation,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SOC}(t)=\mathrm{SOC}(t_0)-\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{app}}}\int_{t_0}^{t} I(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,
\label{eq:lfp_soc_reference}
\end{equation}
with resets to $\mathrm{SOC}=1$ after sustained constant-voltage full-charge phases to reduce long-horizon drift. The SOH label is obtained from periodic capacity check-ups,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SOH}_k=\frac{C_k}{C_{\mathrm{nom}}},
\label{eq:lfp_soh_reference}
\end{equation}
where $C_k$ is the measured discharge capacity and $C_{\mathrm{nom}}$ is the nominal capacity.\autocite{plettBatteryManagementSystems2015,berecibarCriticalReviewState2016} Both labels rely on dataset-side information -- full-capacity discharge tests and sustained CV-based full-charge resets -- that is not available during embedded deployment. They therefore serve as reference labels for model training, estimator calibration or validation, and evaluation in Chapters~6 and~7.

\chapter{Neural Network Architecture for Ageing Estimation in Stationary Storage Systems}
\section{Application Context and Related Work}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item State-of-health estimation is a central prerequisite for reliable stationary storage operation, especially in smart-grid and microgrid settings in which storage units must remain available over long maintenance intervals and under fluctuating renewable-power conditions.\autocite{sahriEnergyManagementSystem2021,solimanSupervisoryEnergyManagement2021}
\item This application context is more demanding than conventional laboratory estimation tasks because smart-grid storage operates under varying load, charging, and renewable-infeed conditions and therefore requires methods that remain informative outside narrowly standardized test windows. The ageing tests considered in this chapter are designed to reflect these operating characteristics in a controlled laboratory setting so that the later findings remain relevant for practical stationary-storage deployment rather than only for idealized benchmark cases.
\item Within this field, the literature usually distinguishes between model-based and data-driven SOH estimation. Model-based methods exploit explicit physical battery descriptions, whereas data-driven methods learn the relation between measured operating variables and the degradation state directly from data. The data-driven route is chosen here because it is better suited to strongly nonlinear ageing behaviour and does not require the same level of electrochemical model knowledge for practical deployment.\autocite{gouStateofHealthEstimationRemainingUsefulLife2020,ojiDataDrivenMethodsBattery2021}
\item Representative model-based alternatives include Kalman-filter-based joint SOC/SOH estimation, particle-filter-based ageing tracking, and fractional-order ageing models. Reported errors in this group range from about \SI{1}{\percent} for dual adaptive Kalman-filter approaches to around \SI{3}{\percent} for capacity-fade estimation and \SI{4}{\percent} for power-fade estimation, while fractional-order-model studies report remaining-capacity errors near \SI{3.1}{\percent}. These approaches can therefore achieve strong nominal accuracy, but they depend on accurate parameter identification, carefully tuned filters, and, in some cases, computationally demanding physical formulations. Their robustness may further deteriorate when the initial SOC is uncertain, when empirical ageing relations do not generalize cleanly across operating regimes, or when offline parameter-identification steps are required before deployment.\autocite{liJointEstimationState2021,biOnlineStateHealth2020,tianFractionalOrderModelBasedIncremental2019}
\item On the measurement-driven and machine-learning side, the discussion covers pressure-based abnormality detection, partial-voltage and incremental-capacity methods, hybrid deep-learning--Kalman combinations, autoregressive prediction, support-vector-machine approaches, fuzzy-entropy features, and SOC-domain preprocessing. Direct-measurement approaches based on reduced charging-voltage windows report average errors below \SI{2.5}{\percent}, and hybrid deep-learning--Kalman combinations reduce estimation errors to about \SI{2}{\percent}. At the same time, these studies reveal typical limitations such as sensitivity to selected operating windows and charging conditions, growing estimation errors at higher degradation levels, dependence on feature validity, susceptibility to abnormal data points, and limited transferability to varying operating conditions.\autocite{ebertDetectionCellstackInhomogeneities2017,stroeStateofHealthEstimationLithiumIon2018,stroeSOHEstimationLMO2018,shiOptimizationStateCharge2021,kimImprovedStateofhealthPrediction2022,xiongOnlineStateofHealthEstimation2021,suiRobustFuzzyEntropyBased2022,joBatteryStateofHealthEstimation2021}
\item More recent hybrid and sequence-based approaches process raw voltage, current, and temperature histories directly by neural networks or combine data-driven inference with physics-informed components. Sequence-to-sequence hybrid degradation prediction has, for example, reported mean absolute percentage errors below \SI{2.5}{\percent} for the capacity trajectory and \SI{6.5}{\percent} for the remaining usable cycles when only \SI{20}{\percent} of the data are used, with further reductions to below \SI{1.3}{\percent} and \SI{5}{\percent} when \SI{50}{\percent} of the data are available. These approaches are methodologically close to the later development path of this dissertation. The present ageing-estimation study nevertheless starts from a deliberately simpler MLP baseline in order to test how far feature engineering and lag-sequence construction can already push a comparatively lightweight model.\autocite{xuNovelHybridPhysicsBased2023,pangPhysicsinformedNeuralNetwork2023,kimEstimationLiionBattery2018,chaouiAgingPredictionState2017}
\item The intention is therefore not to claim that MLPs are universally superior to recurrent or hybrid models. The central assumption is instead that a suitable representation of voltage, temperature, current, and energy history can make a simple feedforward network surprisingly effective while keeping the model easier to train, easier to interpret structurally, and closer to later deployment-oriented questions. This choice also reflects a practical concern: for continuously operated storage systems, classical SOH assessment from clearly delimited capacity tests is often unavailable online, so the estimator must infer degradation from operational trajectories observed in less structured states.\autocite{makridakisStatisticalMachineLearning2018,howStateChargeEstimation2019a,hossainlipuDatadrivenStateCharge2020}
\item The methodological contribution of the chapter lies less in proposing an exotic neural-network architecture than in combining a compact MLP with a preprocessing pipeline tailored to stationary-storage data. In particular, lag-sequence construction allows the network to learn from segmented operating histories instead of relying on explicitly repeated capacity-test signatures, which improves data efficiency and keeps storage and training effort manageable. The additional inclusion of energy as an input variable broadens the information basis beyond the standard trio of current, voltage, and temperature and is intended to improve robustness under heterogeneous operating conditions.
\item Under this framing, the approach is meant as a resilient compromise between accuracy, interpretability, and deployability. It targets imperfect and partially irregular measurement streams, avoids dependence on detailed electrochemical cell models, and is designed to remain transferable in principle across different stationary-storage configurations when sufficient training data are available. The chapter is organized accordingly: it first introduces the dataset and preprocessing logic, then the MLP structure and evaluation setup, and finally the experimental results. The overall workflow discussed in these sections is summarized later in Figure~\ref{fig:paper1_process}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Preprocessing Method and Feature Engineering}
\subsection{Cumulative Current}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The preprocessing pipeline is built around the argument that instantaneous current alone is not sufficient as an explanatory variable for ageing estimation.
\item Cumulative current is introduced as the central preprocessing feature because it captures the two quantities that are most relevant at this stage: the time dependence of the measurements and the cumulative throughput stress experienced by the cell.
\item In its basic form, integrating the absolute current over time encodes not only the present loading level but also the prior electrochemical exposure and, therefore, a compact proxy for the cyclic age that is not visible from an instantaneous sample alone.
\item The formulation is also physically motivated by the interaction between current and voltage over a sequence: their joint evolution carries ageing-relevant information that is closer to an impedance-like gradient than a single current reading. The preprocessing is therefore extended by distinguishing charging and discharging contributions, because both directions can induce different voltage gradients and different effective ageing signatures.
\item The corresponding error reduction can be observed empirically, but because these effects are strongly collinear, the improvement cannot be attributed cleanly to one isolated mechanism alone.
\item In this formulation, cumulative current becomes the feature that allows a relatively simple feedforward network to still access ageing-relevant temporal context without explicitly using a recurrent structure.
\item Voltage and temperature are retained as further direct inputs, so the final feature set combines operating-state information with a compact representation of the prior load exposure.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Formation of the Lag Sequence}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Each learning sample is constructed as a lag sequence of voltage, temperature, and cumulative current, with the SOH at the current point in time used as the corresponding label, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:paper1_lag_sequence}.
\item This transforms the continuous measurement stream into structured sliding-window samples that can be processed by a feedforward network without passing the complete life history of an individual cell directly into the model. The inclusion of past input values is intended to improve prediction quality, while the sliding window defines the temporal duration of the usable history.
\item The initial parameterisation uses a time resolution of \SI{30}{\minute} and 12 lag steps, which corresponds to a history length of \SI{6}{\hour}. This starting choice depends on the expected usage pattern, the charging and discharging durations, and the presence of short high-current phases in the data.
\item The time resolution has to match the actual charging and discharging behaviour. If the spacing is too coarse, relevant dynamics disappear. If it is too fine, the model sees an unnecessarily long and noisy representation that can make training less effective. If short pulses fall within one interval, they are represented only through the averaged feature values of that interval.
\item This design can also be linked to the sampling theorem: the spacing between data points has to remain compatible with the temporal structure of the charging and discharging cycles so that the network can still recognise the relevant periodic behaviour.\autocite{puenteleonSignaleUndSysteme2015}
\item Because the raw measurements are resampled before sequence generation, the preprocessing step also acts like a low-pass filter and strongly attenuates high-frequency noise components. Noise is therefore not treated as a dominant issue for the present estimation setup.
\item A further benefit of the lag-sequence approach is that the training samples are no longer tied to one specific cell trajectory. Instead of memorising individual cells, the model is trained on many local windows drawn from the full dataset.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data Scaling}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item All input features are scaled to the interval between 0 and 1 before training.\autocite{salimansWeightNormalizationSimple2016}
\item This step is necessary because the numerical ranges of the features differ strongly. Voltage remains in a narrow interval between \SI{2.75}{\volt} and \SI{4.2}{\volt}, whereas cumulative current can rise to several thousand ampere-hours across the full ageing experiment.
\item Uniform scaling improves numerical stability, supports convergence during optimisation, and avoids a situation in which large-magnitude inputs dominate the network weights while smaller but still relevant signals are effectively ignored.\autocite{salimansWeightNormalizationSimple2016}
\item The same scaling convention must be applied consistently to the training, validation, and test data so that the learned mapping is evaluated under comparable feature ranges and the resulting performance metrics remain interpretable across all splits.
\end{itemize}

\section{Multilayer Perceptron Neural Network Model Structure}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The selected model type is a multilayer perceptron with fully connected layers.
\item A convolutional interpretation is not pursued because the data do not exhibit a spatial image-like structure. Recurrent architectures such as LSTMs are also not used at this stage, because the temporal dependence is already encoded through the lag-sequence construction and because the recurrent alternatives are more resource-intensive and more difficult to tune in the chosen setting.\autocite{weytjensCashFlowPrediction2021}
\item This architecture choice is also connected to the later hyperparameter-search setup: the chapter keeps the estimator architecture feedforward and shifts the main optimization effort to lag-window design, network depth, node count, and training parameters, which are then searched efficiently with Hyperband.\autocite{liHyperbandNovelBanditBased2016}
\item As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:paper1_mlp_architecture}, the MLP consists of an input layer, several dense hidden layers with ReLU activation, and a single linear output node for the SOH estimate.
\item The original starting point is intentionally small and uses one hidden layer with eight nodes. The final network depth and width are then determined in the hyperparameter search.
\item In the tuned configuration, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:paper1_mlp_config}, the model grows into a deep dense architecture with nine hidden/output stages and a final linear regression output. The resulting structure remains conceptually simple from an architectural point of view, but it is substantially larger than the initial baseline.
\end{itemize}

\section{Configuration and Hyperparameter Tuning of the Neural Network}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item This section describes the transition from the initial small MLP baseline to the tuned final network, with the resulting architecture summarised in Table~\ref{tab:paper1_mlp_config}.
\item Model quality is evaluated by means of mean squared error and mean absolute error.
\item Mean squared error is used as the central optimisation-oriented metric during training and validation, whereas both MSE and MAE are used for the final interpretation on the held-out test cells. The original ageing-estimation study reports errors in percentage points of SOH; for consistency with the later chapters, the corresponding normalized SOH error is obtained by dividing MAE values by 100 and MSE values by 10{,}000.
\item The hyperparameter search covers the number of lag steps, the temporal resolution of the lag sequence, network depth, number of nodes, batch size, kernel regularisation, optimizer type, and activation function. Within this set, the number of lag steps and the temporal resolution are the most method-specific parameters, because they define how much history is provided to the model and at which temporal granularity it is represented.
\item The number of nodes controls the representational capacity of the network, whereas the network depth influences how strongly the model can build more abstract internal feature combinations across layers. Both parameters therefore affect the balance between flexibility, generalisation, and model complexity.
\item Hyperband is used as the main search strategy because it distributes training resources adaptively, evaluates many candidate settings quickly, and allocates more epochs only to the most promising configurations.\autocite{liHyperbandNovelBanditBased2016}
\item The study reports that ADAM outperforms RMSProp, Adadelta, and Adagrad for this task. Larger batch sizes reduce overfitting tendencies, whereas explicit kernel regularisation does not lead to a meaningful improvement.
\item Batch size also affects the training trade-off directly: larger batches are computationally efficient and converge reliably, whereas smaller batches can improve generalisation at the cost of longer or less stable optimisation.
\item The optimizer analysis is complemented by an activation-function comparison. ReLU yields the lowest loss in the tested setup, while sigmoid, tanh, and softmax perform worse.
\item The models are trained on approximately 15{,}000 sequences with varying batch-size and epoch combinations, and larger batches converge to a comparable minimum while reducing overfitting tendencies.
\end{itemize}

\section{Interpretation of the Ageing-Estimation Results}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item A meaningful SOH estimate can be obtained with a comparatively simple feedforward model when the preprocessing and feature engineering are aligned with the underlying ageing problem.
\item One of the central insights is that the input representation is at least as important as the model class itself. Cumulative-current features, lag-sequence construction, and the explicit reconstruction of the SOH target are decisive parts of the overall method and form the actual methodological core of this chapter.
\item The results simultaneously show the dependence of neural SOH estimation on the available dataset. Atypical ageing trajectories, interrupted tests, and limited data diversity remain the dominant constraints on generalisation.
\item Even under these limitations, the approach reaches a level of accuracy that remains competitive with more complex alternatives. Within the dissertation, this chapter should therefore not be read as a claim that feedforward MLPs are universally preferable to recurrent models. Its role is to establish a first data-driven baseline in which ageing-relevant temporal information is represented explicitly while the estimator architecture itself is kept deliberately simple.
\item The method is not restricted to one narrowly defined technology, because it relies only on directly measured quantities such as voltage, temperature, and current-derived throughput features. In principle, the concept can therefore be transferred to other cells and chemistries if sufficiently rich data are available.
\item This baseline role is important for the broader argument of the dissertation. Once a workable representation for ageing information has been identified, the next question is no longer only how well the estimator performs under nominal conditions, but how different estimator classes behave under deployment-relevant disturbances such as bias, noise, missing samples, and initialization errors. This motivates the shift toward the robustness benchmark in the following chapter.
\item Building on this robustness perspective, the later chapters extend the analysis to recurrent LSTM--MLP models for SOC and SOH estimation. There, the aim is no longer only to compare estimator concepts at a general level, but to examine whether stateful recurrent models offer advantages when temporal information has to be processed causally over long streaming sequences and ultimately transferred to embedded microcontroller execution.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Robustness Benchmarking of Embedded Battery State Estimation}
\section{Motivation and practical relevance}
This chapter investigates deployment-facing battery-state estimation under realistic Battery Management System conditions rather than under clean laboratory input signals alone. Reliable SOC and SOH estimation is a core BMS function because it directly shapes safety margins, usable energy, operating limits, charge control, and degradation-aware management in both mobile and stationary battery systems.\autocite{shrivastavaReviewTechnologicalAdvancement2023,guoSystematicReviewElectrochemical2024,yaoSmarterBatteryManagement2025b} Practical deployment quality is not defined by clean-condition accuracy alone, however, because real BMS operation is exposed to sensor bias, additive noise, missing samples, irregular timing, restart-related initialization mismatch, and transient signal spikes caused by the measurement chain or by communication faults.\autocite{liRobustnessSOCEstimation2015,naseriReliableWirelessBMS2025,bergerBenchmarkingBatteryManagement2024}

In the requirement framework introduced in Figure~\ref{fig:bms_requirements}, this chapter therefore occupies the performance branch of the dissertation. The focus is not a full production-BMS validation campaign, but the question of how estimator classes behave when realistic measurement-side disturbances are applied under otherwise matched conditions. Estimator quality is accordingly interpreted as a three-part performance problem consisting of nominal baseline accuracy, convergence behavior after state inconsistency, and robustness under disturbed measurements and measurement-continuity faults.

\section{State of the art in battery state estimation}
The relevant literature usually distinguishes between direct-measurement or integration-based estimators, typically centered on Coulomb counting, model-based estimators built around equivalent-circuit, observer-based, or electrochemical structure, hybrid approaches that combine physical structure with learned adaptation, and purely data-driven recurrent or sequence models.\autocite{khanComparativeStudyDifferent2022,shrivastavaReviewTechnologicalAdvancement2023,guoSystematicReviewElectrochemical2024,yaoSmarterBatteryManagement2025b} Direct-measurement approaches remain attractive because they are simple, computationally inexpensive, and easy to deploy, but they are structurally exposed to current bias, missing data, and initialization errors because they do not contain an internal correction mechanism.\autocite{khanComparativeStudyDifferent2022,liRobustnessSOCEstimation2015}

Equivalent-circuit and observer-based estimators offer physical interpretability and voltage-based correction capability, but their practical performance depends strongly on parameter quality, operating-point observability, and measurement quality.\autocite{gaoEnhancedStateofchargeEstimation2022,guoSystematicReviewElectrochemical2024,liRobustnessSOCEstimation2015} Hybrid approaches combine physical state propagation with learned adaptation of capacity, parameters, or auxiliary states and are therefore attractive for joint SOC/SOH estimation;\autocite{gaoCoEstimationStateofChargeStateof2022,shenAlternativeCombinedCoestimation2022,wangFusionEstimationLithiumion2022} such combinations can improve baseline calibration, but they do not automatically remove structural sensitivity to measurement disturbances. Data-driven recurrent estimators can exploit nonlinear relations between voltage, current, temperature, and derived online variables, and more recent work has also shown that such models can be prepared for embedded deployment through TinyML-oriented implementations, pruning, and quantization.\autocite{mondalEstimatingStateofChargeLithiumIon2024,Embedded_AI_only_SOC_mobile,giazitzisCaseStudyTiny2024,mazziStateChargeEstimation2022,yaoSmarterBatteryManagement2025b}

\section{Performance and embedded deployment gap}
The main benchmark gap is not only that much of the literature still emphasizes clean-data accuracy, average error metrics, or computational efficiency, but also that comparatively fewer studies analyze estimator behavior under realistic measurement faults and timing disturbances.\autocite{shrivastavaReviewTechnologicalAdvancement2023,Embedded_AI_only_SOC_mobile,giazitzisCaseStudyTiny2024,mazziStateChargeEstimation2022} Where robustness is studied, it often remains confined to a single estimator family, so cross-family comparisons under the same disturbance protocol and under comparable embedded-ready execution conditions are still limited.\autocite{liRobustnessSOCEstimation2015,naseriReliableWirelessBMS2025}

Broader BMS benchmarking studies have highlighted the importance of reproducible scenario design and deployment-relevant validation, but they do not by themselves close the gap between clean-data estimator comparison and measurement-level performance benchmarking across fundamentally different estimator classes.\autocite{bergerBenchmarkingBatteryManagement2024} This gap is especially relevant in embedded BMS design, where estimator selection must jointly consider nominal accuracy, convergence after inconsistency, robustness under disturbed measurements, and computational constraints.

\section{Contribution and study objectives}
The central objective is a measurement-only robustness benchmark in which estimator performance is isolated from broader system-level effects such as hardware-resource optimization or implementation-effort comparison. The benchmark is deliberately restricted to disturbances applied only to measurable inputs and their timing, including quantization effects, bias, additive noise, missing samples, irregular sampling, burst dropout, voltage spikes, and initialization mismatch. This design keeps the scenarios physically interpretable and allows the resulting failure modes to be compared under reproducible conditions.

The chapter compares four embedded-ready estimator classes on the same LFP ageing trajectory and under the same disturbance protocol: a direct-measurement model (DM), a hybrid direct-measurement model with learned SOH support (HDM), a hybrid equivalent-circuit model with voltage feedback (HECM), and a data-driven recurrent model with GRU-based SOC estimation and LSTM-based online SOH context (DD). Under clean conditions, HDM provides the lowest baseline error with $\mathrm{MAE}=0.0024$ (0.24 percentage points), followed by HECM with $0.0090$, DD with $0.0100$, and DM with $0.0311$. Under disturbed measurements, however, the ranking changes substantially, which is the core reason for the benchmark. The benchmark should therefore be read as a controlled cross-class comparison that isolates estimator-side failure modes under matched data, matched disturbance definitions, and matched online execution rules. Its purpose is not to identify one universal winner, but to expose complementary strengths and weaknesses across estimator families and to translate them into decision-oriented guidance for embedded BMS design.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Estimator classes under test}
Figure~\ref{fig:robustness_methodology} summarizes the benchmark chain from measured signals to online feature reconstruction, disturbance injection, estimator execution, and global or local performance evaluation. The comparison covers four estimator classes with deliberately different correction mechanisms: DM is the fixed-capacity Coulomb-counting reference, HDM adds capacity adaptation through the shared online SOH estimate, HECM adds voltage-residual feedback through a SOH-aware two-RC observer,\autocite{gaoEnhancedStateofchargeEstimation2022,guoSystematicReviewElectrochemical2024} and DD represents the compact recurrent neural estimator with stateful GRU-based SOC prediction.\autocite{mondalEstimatingStateofChargeLithiumIon2024,yaoSmarterBatteryManagement2025b}

HDM, HECM, and DD all use the same LSTM-based online SOH estimator. The benchmark therefore compares how the SOC estimators use a common health context instead of comparing different SOH backbones. The causal online quantities required by several estimators, especially cumulative charge $Q_c$, equivalent full cycles $\mathrm{EFC}$, derivatives, and hourly SOH aggregates, are reconstructed from the measured stream during benchmark execution. This prevents precomputed dataset-side features from entering only one estimator class.

\subsection{SOC estimation methods}
\subsubsection{Direct-measurement model (DM)}
The overview in Figure~\ref{fig:robustness_methodology} is implemented through four estimator variants that differ in their SOC correction mechanism, but share the same causal measurement stream. The direct measurement branch first constructs the cumulative charge state
\begin{equation}
    Q_c(k)=Q_c(k-1)+\frac{I_k\Delta t_k}{3600\,\mathrm{s\,h^{-1}}},
    \label{eq:robustness_qc_update}
\end{equation}
where $I_k$ is the measured current and $\Delta t_k$ is the elapsed time between two samples. The division by $3600\,\mathrm{s\,h^{-1}}$ converts the current-time product into ampere-hours. In the DM estimator this charge state is mapped to SOC with a fixed nominal capacity,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mathrm{SOC}}_{\mathrm{DM}}(k)
    =
    \mathrm{clip}\!\left(1+\frac{Q_c(k)}{C_{\mathrm{nom}}},0,1\right).
    \label{eq:robustness_soc_dm}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Hybrid direct-measurement model (HDM)}
The same integration core is also used in the HDM estimator. The difference is that the denominator is updated through the shared online SOH estimate,
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{eff}}(k)=C_{\mathrm{nom}}\cdot\widehat{\mathrm{SOH}}(k),
    \label{eq:robustness_ceff}
\end{equation}
which gives the hybrid direct-measurement SOC estimate
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mathrm{SOC}}_{\mathrm{HDM}}(k)
    =
    \mathrm{clip}\!\left(1+\frac{Q_c(k)}{C_{\mathrm{eff}}(k)},0,1\right).
    \label{eq:robustness_soc_hdm}
\end{equation}
DM and HDM therefore differ only in the assumed usable capacity. Both variants keep the same constant-voltage full-charge reset logic: if the upper-voltage condition is sustained for the configured duration, the internal charge state is reset to $Q_c=0$ and the SOC estimate is anchored at full charge.

\subsubsection{Hybrid equivalent-circuit model (HECM)}
The HECM extends the current-driven charge balance with voltage-residual correction. It is implemented as a two-RC observer with state vector
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_k=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \widehat{\mathrm{SOC}}_k & U_{1,k} & U_{2,k}
    \end{bmatrix}^{\top},
    \label{eq:robustness_hecm_state}
\end{equation}
where $U_{1,k}$ and $U_{2,k}$ denote the polarization voltages of the two RC branches. The prediction step uses the previous current sample and propagates the state according to
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_{k}^{-}=\mathbf{A}_k\mathbf{x}_{k-1}+\mathbf{b}_kI_{k-1},
    \label{eq:robustness_hecm_prediction}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A}_k=
    \mathrm{diag}\!\left(1,e^{-\Delta t_k/\tau_1},e^{-\Delta t_k/\tau_2}\right),
    \qquad
    \mathbf{b}_k=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \eta\Delta t_k/C_b\\
        R_1\!\left(1-e^{-\Delta t_k/\tau_1}\right)\\
        R_2\!\left(1-e^{-\Delta t_k/\tau_2}\right)
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:robustness_hecm_ab}
\end{equation}
Here $C_b=C_0\widehat{\mathrm{SOH}}$ is the SOH-scaled capacity used by the observer. The voltage prediction used for the correction step is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{U}_k
    =
    \mathrm{OCV}\!\left(\widehat{\mathrm{SOC}}_k,\widehat{\mathrm{SOH}}_k,I_k\right)
    +U_{1,k}+U_{2,k}+R_iI_k .
    \label{eq:robustness_hecm_voltage}
\end{equation}
The Kalman correction then uses the residual between this predicted terminal voltage and the measured voltage to update the internal state. The parameters $R_i$, $R_1$, $R_2$, $\tau_1$, $\tau_2$, $\mathrm{OCV}$, and $\mathrm{dOCV}/\mathrm{dSOC}$ are not treated as constants; they are interpolated from pre-identified SOC/SOH lookup surfaces with separate charge and discharge parameterizations. This is the main structural reason why HECM can re-anchor the integrated SOC estimate through voltage information while still using the same online SOH context as HDM and DD.

\subsubsection{Data-driven SOC model (DD)}
The DD estimator uses a stateful GRU instead of an explicit observer equation. At each online time step it receives the feature vector
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_k=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        U_k & I_k & T_k & \widehat{\mathrm{SOH}}_k & Q_c(k) &
        \frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}t}(k) &
        \frac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}t}(k) &
        \Delta t_k
    \end{bmatrix}^{\top}.
    \label{eq:robustness_dd_feature_vector}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:robustness_dd_architecture}(a) shows the block structure of this model. In its floating-point base configuration, a single GRU layer with hidden size 96 processes the eight-channel feature vector; an MLP head with one hidden layer of size 96, ReLU activation, and a sigmoid output then maps the recurrent state to a SOC estimate constrained to $[0,1]$. The benchmark itself uses the deployment-prepared variant of this model, in which structured pruning reduces the hidden size from 96 to 67 and the weights are stored as INT8 values (Section~\ref{sec:robustness_embedded_preparation}). During training, the recurrent core is exposed to causal sequence segments of
\begin{equation}
    L_{\mathrm{SOC}}=2024
    \label{eq:robustness_soc_sequence_length}
\end{equation}
consecutive time steps. During benchmark execution the same model is run statefully and causally, so the hidden state is carried forward sample by sample and the MLP head produces a continuous SOC trajectory without access to future samples.

The derivative channels in the DD input are rebuilt online from the disturbed measurement stream,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}t}(k)=\frac{I_k-I_{k-1}}{\Delta t_k},
    \qquad
    \frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}t}(k)=\frac{U_k-U_{k-1}}{\Delta t_k}.
    \label{eq:robustness_derivative_features}
\end{equation}
Together with the explicit $\Delta t_k$ channel, these quantities allow the neural model to distinguish local voltage or current dynamics from timing changes under irregular sampling. The inclusion of $Q_c(k)$ also ensures that the DD model receives the same charge-throughput information that drives DM and HDM.\autocite{mondalEstimatingStateofChargeLithiumIon2024,yaoSmarterBatteryManagement2025b}

\subsection{SOH estimation method}
HDM, HECM, and DD all use the same LSTM-based SOH estimator. Its input is an hourly feature vector obtained from the online channels $\{U,I,T,\mathrm{EFC},Q_c\}$ by computing mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum for each channel. The five base channels therefore form a 20-dimensional hourly input vector.\autocite{Embedded_AI_SOH_badData,gouEnsembleLearningBasedDataDriven2021,liOnlineCapacityEstimation2021,rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a}

Figure~\ref{fig:robustness_dd_architecture}(b) summarizes the architecture of this network. It consists of four sequential stages.

The first stage is a \textbf{feature projection block}. The term \emph{projection} is used deliberately here to avoid confusion with hardware \emph{embedding} discussed elsewhere in the dissertation; the function of this block is a learned transformation of the 20-dimensional statistical input into a compact, task-adapted representation before the recurrent processing begins. This is necessary because the 20 input values are heterogeneous statistics -- means, standard deviations, minima, and maxima -- drawn from physically different channels with different units and scales. The projection block consists of two successive linear layers of width 128 with layer normalisation and GELU activation between and after them. Each linear layer is a full matrix multiplication with its own trainable weights, so the first layer learns which combinations of the 20 input statistics are jointly relevant for SOH, and the second layer refines these combinations further. The GELU activation function is used instead of ReLU because it is smooth and differentiable everywhere, which supports gradient flow in deeper networks without the dying-neuron effect of hard-clipping negative values to zero; this choice is consistent with the residual-style architecture that follows.\autocite{hendrycksGaussianErrorLinear2016}

The second stage is a \textbf{three-layer unidirectional LSTM} with hidden size 192. Three stacked LSTM layers are used because SOH evolves over long time horizons -- weeks to months of hourly aggregates -- and a hierarchical recurrent structure allows the first layer to capture short-term variation patterns, the second layer to aggregate these into medium-term trends, and the third layer to distil the slowest degradation dynamics that unfold over weeks to months. A single layer would conflate all these timescales in one set of weights. The network is strictly unidirectional: the hidden state at each hourly step accumulates information only from past observations, with no access to future measurements, which is a hard requirement for deployment in a continuously running BMS.

The third stage consists of \textbf{two residual MLP blocks}. Each block receives the 192-dimensional LSTM output as input $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{192}$ and computes
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{y} = \mathrm{LayerNorm}\!\left(\mathbf{x} + \mathrm{Dropout}\!\left(W_2\,\mathrm{GELU}(W_1\mathbf{x})\right)\right),
    \label{eq:residual_block}
\end{equation}
where $W_1\in\mathbb{R}^{160\times 192}$ contracts the input to an internal width of 160 and $W_2\in\mathbb{R}^{192\times 160}$ expands it back to 192. The key property is the \emph{skip connection}: the original LSTM output $\mathbf{x}$ is added directly to the transformed output before normalisation. As a result, the block does not have to learn the full output from scratch; instead it learns only the \emph{residual correction} -- the difference between what the LSTM already computed and a better representation. This protects the useful temporal information already contained in the LSTM output: in the worst case, the block weights converge toward zero and the signal passes through unchanged. Without the skip connection, the block could overwrite the LSTM output entirely, which would waste the recurrent computation. The input and output dimension stays at 192 throughout both blocks; they refine the representation without compressing it.

The fourth stage is the \textbf{prediction head}, a three-layer MLP that maps the 192-dimensional LSTM output to a single scalar SOH estimate: a first layer reduces 192 to 160, a second hidden layer stays at width 160, both with GELU activation and dropout, followed by a final linear layer with a single output neuron. There is no skip connection here because the purpose of this stage is precisely to compress the high-dimensional recurrent state into a scalar; the final linear output is unconstrained so that the network can represent the full SOH range without saturation effects.

Three width values appear in this section with distinct roles: 128 is the feature-projection width, 192 is the LSTM hidden size, and 160 is the shared internal width of the residual blocks and the prediction head. The base model has approximately 1\,041\,000 trainable parameters and is deployed with INT8 weight quantization (Section~\ref{sec:robustness_embedded_preparation}). In online execution, only one hourly aggregate is processed at a time; the hidden and cell states are carried forward step by step using only past observations, the first output is anchored with the configured initial SOH value, and later estimates are held piecewise constant between hourly updates. This common SOH context is consumed differently by the SOC estimators: HDM uses it through $C_{\mathrm{eff}}$, HECM uses it in the observer capacity and lookup scheduling, and DD receives it as part of its recurrent feature vector.

\subsection{Embedded-oriented model preparation}
\label{sec:robustness_embedded_preparation}
All compared estimators are constrained to a common STM32H755ZI-class reference platform with \SI{2}{MB} dual-bank flash (\SI{1}{MB} per bank) and \SI{1}{MB} RAM. This shared feasibility criterion keeps the comparison deployment-grounded: it ensures that all performance differences are interpretable within a common hardware class and prevents an asymmetric comparison between compact analytical estimators and neural models that would not be feasible on the same platform.

DM requires no compression: its four persistent scalar states occupy 4.2 KiB of flash. HECM also requires no neural-weight compression, but its flash footprint is dominated by the embedded ECM parameter lookup tables -- two SOH$\times$SOC grids of seven electrical parameters (internal resistance, RC time constants, open-circuit voltage, and its derivative) for the charge and discharge directions, stored as float32 constants -- bringing its flash total to 442.5 KiB and making it the largest single component. The recurrent components used by HDM and DD are deployment-prepared variants. For the SOC-GRU, structured width reduction and finetuning reduce the hidden size from 96 to 67, followed by INT8 weight quantization. For the SOH-LSTM, structured pruning at the applied intensity collapses model accuracy entirely, so this component is deployed in its base form with INT8 weight quantization only, retaining all 192 hidden units across three layers. The resulting deployment-ready hidden sizes are therefore 67 for the SOC-GRU and 192 for the SOH-LSTM. The SOH-LSTM in INT8 carries approximately 1\,034 KiB of quantized weight data plus a further 29 KiB of float32 bias and normalisation parameters, for a total of approximately 1\,038 KiB -- occupying essentially one full \SI{1}{MB} flash bank on its own. The remaining components (SOC-GRU at 27.9 KiB, DM at 4.2 KiB, HECM at 442.5 KiB) fit together in the second bank. Table~\ref{tab:robust_component_footprints} lists the reusable building blocks; when these components are combined to form DM, HDM, HECM, and DD, all four estimators remain within the \SI{2}{MB} dual-bank flash capacity of the STM32H755ZI reference platform.

\subsection{Performance benchmark design}
The benchmark perturbs only signals that a BMS controller would actually receive from its sensors: current, voltage, temperature, sample availability, and sampling timing. Model-internal quantities -- such as LSTM hidden states or the model weights themselves -- are never modified directly. This restriction keeps all disturbance scenarios physically interpretable: every failure mode tested corresponds to something that can go wrong at the sensor, wiring, or communication layer of a real embedded system, rather than to artificial tampering with the estimator internals.\autocite{bergerBenchmarkingBatteryManagement2024}

Figure~\ref{fig:robustness_disturbance_taxonomy} separates the resulting cases into input disturbances, initialization mismatch, and measurement-continuity faults, meaning disturbances of sample availability, sample timing, or frozen data updates. The numerical levels in Table~\ref{tab:robustness_scenario_protocol} combine three roles: realistic acquisition limits, adverse but plausible measurement disturbances, and deliberate stress cases for recovery analysis.\autocite{BQ7961616SeriesBattery2023,DesignConsiderationsCurrent,TMCS11083Functional,TLE4972HighPrecision,aielloElectromagneticSusceptibilityBattery2020,liuAssessingImpactSampling2025} During every run, all four estimators receive the identical disturbed sensor time series -- the same modified sequence of voltage, current, temperature, and timing samples -- so that any difference in output is attributable to estimator structure rather than to differences in the input. Each estimator then reconstructs its auxiliary quantities (cumulative charge $Q_c$, equivalent full cycles $\mathrm{EFC}$, current derivative, hourly SOH aggregates) causally and online from this disturbed time series, not from precomputed clean dataset columns. A sensor bias therefore propagates into the derived features exactly as it would in a real BMS, which keeps the comparison physically consistent across all estimator classes.

\subsection{Metrics and evaluation criteria}
\label{sec:robustness_metrics}
The benchmark evaluates three performance dimensions: nominal accuracy, convergence behavior after state inconsistency, and robustness under both longer-lasting and short-term measurement disturbances. These three dimensions are not independent post-hoc labels, but the aggregation logic used later in the result interpretation and in the decision-oriented synthesis. Nominal accuracy is read from the clean baseline run, robustness is read from the additional error caused by each disturbed scenario relative to that estimator's own baseline, and convergence is read from the time needed to return to a baseline-related error band after an imposed inconsistency or interrupted signal stream.

For each estimator $m$ and disturbed scenario $s$, the main global robustness penalty is therefore defined as
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\mathrm{MAE}_{m,s}
    =
    \mathrm{MAE}_{m,s}
    -
    \mathrm{MAE}_{m,\mathrm{base}} .
    \label{eq:robustness_delta_mae}
\end{equation}
Positive values indicate that the disturbance degrades the estimator relative to its own clean-operation reference; values close to zero indicate that the disturbance does not materially change the full-trajectory average error. Global disturbed-operation performance is quantified through MAE, RMSE, bias, maximum error, and the $95$th percentile absolute error. MAE and RMSE describe the average error level, bias describes systematic over- or underestimation, the maximum error reports the single largest deviation, and P95 is the absolute-error value that \SI{95}{\percent} of all evaluated samples do not exceed. P95 therefore describes the larger-error range of the trajectory without being determined by the single worst sample.

Local disturbance performance is additionally analysed through scenario-specific quantities such as jump counts, output variance, absolute-error variance, excess rolling MAE, recovery time, and aligned post-disturbance error, because several scenarios do not primarily manifest as a collapse of full-run MAE but as short-term output fluctuations, delayed drift, or slow re-synchronization. For scenarios in which the affected samples or time interval are explicitly known, the analysis separates the trajectory into pre-disturbance, disturbance, and post-disturbance phases. This makes it possible to quantify error growth during the disturbance, recovery time after the event, and residual error after the input stream has returned to normal. In the current benchmark phase, ADC quantization, current bias, voltage noise, temperature noise, missing samples, and irregular sampling are interpreted mainly through global metrics, whereas burst dropout, voltage spikes, current-noise detail, and initialization mismatch additionally require local evidence because their most relevant effects are episodic, recovery-related, or strongly localized in time.

Recovery after initialization errors is interpreted through two baseline-relative operating bands rather than through one universal fixed threshold. This keeps the recovery criterion tied to the normal clean-condition performance level of each estimator and avoids judging models with very different baseline accuracy against the same absolute tolerance. The strict band is defined as
\begin{equation}
    B_{\mathrm{strict}}
    =
    \max\!\left(\mathrm{P95}_{\mathrm{base}},\,1.5\cdot\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{base}}\right).
    \label{eq:robustness_strict_band}
\end{equation}
In the strict criterion, $\mathrm{P95}_{\mathrm{base}}$ ensures that the recovery band includes the larger errors that already occur during clean baseline operation, while $1.5 \cdot \mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{base}}$ provides a minimum margin around the average baseline error. Taking the maximum of both terms means that recovery is judged against the more tolerant of these two baseline-derived limits, so recovery under this band means re-entry close to the normal baseline regime without imposing unrealistically narrow limits. The fair band is
\begin{equation}
    B_{\mathrm{fair}}
    =
    \max\!\left(2\cdot\mathrm{P95}_{\mathrm{base}},\,3\cdot\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{base}},\,0.02\right),
    \label{eq:robustness_fair_band}
\end{equation}
and acts as a more deployment-tolerant criterion for estimators that re-enter a practically acceptable operating range without immediately returning to their strict near-baseline regime. The lower bound of $0.02$ avoids unrealistically tight fair-band thresholds for very accurate baseline models. Both recovery bands require sustained re-entry over a fixed horizon so that the reported recovery times reflect stable resynchronization rather than an accidental short crossing of the threshold.

Reporting both bands avoids making the recovery conclusion depend on one manually chosen limit. If an estimator returns only to the fair band, it has reached a practically acceptable range but not its near-baseline regime; if it also returns to the strict band, the recovery is stronger. This multi-scale metric design therefore evaluates performance as a structured combination of nominal accuracy, convergence behavior, global degradation, local disturbance response, and long-term drift.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Dataset and data acquisition}
The benchmark uses the LFP DoE Cycle Ageing Dataset described in Section~\ref{sec:datasets}.\autocite{lfpDoeCycleAgingDataset2025} It provides high-resolution ageing-test records from repeated cycling and check-up phases that extend from early-life operation into low-SOH operation, including sections below the common \SI{80}{\percent} end-of-life reference level. This makes the dataset suitable for reproducible disturbance injection and evaluation-label construction over a broad degradation range. In the current benchmark phase, all estimator classes are evaluated on the same representative ageing trajectory from one LFP cell and under the same disturbance protocol so that performance differences can be attributed to estimator structure under matched data and label definitions before extending the study to additional cells and cross-cell validation.

The available raw channels are current, voltage, temperature, and time. Feature engineering converts these measurements into two separate data types: evaluation labels and causal online features. The labels are derived from capacity-test intervals and physically motivated SOC reset criteria and contain the capacity, SOH, and SOC traces used for benchmark scoring. The online features are reconstructed during each benchmark run from the same input stream that each estimator receives; they include the cumulative charge state $Q_c$, equivalent full cycles $\mathrm{EFC}$, derivative channels such as $dU/dt$ and $dI/dt$, and hourly SOH-context aggregates. For these hourly aggregates, the online channels $\{U,I,T,\mathrm{EFC},Q_c\}$ are summarized by mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum.

\subsection{Evaluation labels and online feature reconstruction}
For label construction, the signed transferred charge is computed sample-wise as
\begin{equation}
    Q_k=\frac{I_k\Delta t_k}{3600},
    \label{eq:robustness_reference_charge_increment}
\end{equation}
and the capacity label used as evaluation reference is obtained from the absolute transferred charge during dedicated capacity-test intervals,
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{ref}}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{cap}}}\left|Q_k\right|.
    \label{eq:robustness_reference_capacity}
\end{equation}
The SOH label used as evaluation reference is then defined as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SOH}_{\mathrm{ref}}=\frac{C_{\mathrm{ref}}}{C_{\mathrm{ref},0}},
    \label{eq:robustness_reference_soh}
\end{equation}
where $C_{\mathrm{ref},0}$ denotes the first measured reference capacity of the trajectory, and the corresponding capacity and SOH columns are interpolated between the dedicated capacity-test intervals. The SOC label used as evaluation reference follows from Coulomb counting,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{ref}}=1+\frac{Q_c}{C_{\mathrm{ref},0}}.
    \label{eq:robustness_reference_soc}
\end{equation}
During preprocessing, the cumulative charge state is reset to $Q_c=0$ only after the voltage has remained close to the upper voltage limit for the configured CV duration; in the benchmark implementation this duration is \SI{300}{\second}. This prevents a short voltage excursion from being interpreted as a full-charge point. At the lower-voltage limit, $Q_c$ is reset to $-C_{\mathrm{ref},0}$.

In the benchmark implementation, the online EFC feature is reconstructed from absolute charge throughput,
\begin{equation}
    Q_{\mathrm{thr}}(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{|I_j|\Delta t_j}{3600},
    \qquad
    \mathrm{EFC}(k)=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{thr}}(k)}{C_{\mathrm{nom}}}.
    \label{eq:robustness_efc}
\end{equation}
Here $C_{\mathrm{nom}}$ is the nominal cell capacity used in the benchmark feature pipeline. Unlike the SOC and SOH labels, $\mathrm{EFC}$ is an online feature and is therefore recomputed from the current and timing stream of each benchmark run.

This distinction between labels and online features is central to the benchmark. The labels remain fixed reference values for scoring the online estimates, whereas $Q_c$, $\mathrm{EFC}$, $dU/dt$, $dI/dt$, and the hourly aggregates are rebuilt from the same clean or disturbed inputs that are passed to the estimators. Consequently, disturbed measurements affect the estimator inputs and derived features, but not the reference labels used for scoring.

\subsection{Test trajectory and benchmark scenarios}
All estimator classes are run on the same benchmark trajectory and the same scenario protocol from Table~\ref{tab:robustness_scenario_protocol}. This ensures that differences in the results are attributable to estimator structure and disturbance propagation rather than to different data splits or label definitions. The reported scenarios cover the nominal baseline, current bias, additive current noise, voltage noise, temperature noise, initial SOC mismatch, missing samples, irregular sampling, burst dropout, voltage spikes, and ADC quantization. Together they probe long-term integration drift, voltage-correction sensitivity, disturbance propagation through derived features, frozen updates, timing uncertainty, and local post-event recovery. Input disturbances are applied directly to measured channels, initialization errors are injected only where structurally meaningful, and continuity/timing cases manipulate sample availability or timing while preserving online execution logic.

\subsection{Implementation details}
All compared estimators are executed under online conditions. Disturbed inputs are processed sample by sample, and the required auxiliary quantities are rebuilt directly from the disturbed signals rather than copied from precomputed dataset columns. This applies in particular to $Q_c$, $\mathrm{EFC}$, $dU/dt$, $dI/dt$, and the hourly SOH aggregates, so that no hidden dataset-side variables leak into the benchmark runs.

For the recurrent neural estimators, the benchmark uses the intended stateful embedded execution mode. The SOH-LSTM carries its hidden and cell states from one hourly update to the next, while the SOC-GRU preserves its recurrent state along the sample-wise measurement stream. The neural variants used in the benchmark correspond to the compact pruned-and-quantized architectures introduced above. In freeze and dropout scenarios, disturbance propagation is handled consistently by freezing or distorting the derived online features together with the corresponding measured inputs, which is necessary for a physically meaningful comparison between model classes.

The direct-measurement estimators include the same sustained-CV full-charge reset criterion that is also used to define the full-charge reference point in preprocessing, whereas the data-driven initial-SOC test is implemented through an initial offset of the online $Q_c$ feature because the neural estimator does not expose an explicit SOC state variable. The simulation environment therefore keeps the base nominal capacity used for online feature reconstruction fixed across all estimator classes, while all models receive the same disturbed input streams and are evaluated against the same reference labels and global and local metrics. This is one of the main reasons why the benchmark remains close to later embedded deployment: the estimators are not evaluated as offline regressors, but as causal state estimators under matched measurement-side constraints.

In the compact benchmark matrix, the high-current-noise case uses $\sigma_I=\SI{0.10}{A}$; the additional sweep in Figure~\ref{fig:robustness_noise} extends beyond this value to make local output-variability trends visible. Temperature noise with $\sigma_T=\SI{1.0}{\celsius}$ is weaker in the global metrics, but it still affects the SOH-aware paths because temperature enters the learned health context. \subsection{Initialization error and recovery}
Initialization mismatch is a recovery problem rather than a simple full-run MAE problem, because the relevant question is whether the estimator can re-synchronize after starting from a wrong SOC value. The benchmark therefore uses a reset-free evaluation window and injects an initial mismatch of $-0.10$ on the normalized SOC scale, meaning that the online estimate starts 10 percentage points too low. This disturbance represents a deliberately visible restart inconsistency, for example after missing recent history or weak anchoring of the initial SOC. For DD, the mismatch is implemented as an offset of the online cumulative-charge feature instead of an artificial reset of an inaccessible latent state, which keeps the test aligned with deployment-time observables.

Recovery is evaluated with the strict and fair baseline-related bands defined in Section~\ref{sec:robustness_metrics}. These thresholds are not times and not fixed SOC setpoints; they are admissible absolute SOC errors on the normalized 0--1 scale. They differ between estimator classes because they are computed from each estimator's own clean-condition MAE and P95 error. The strict band marks return to the near-baseline regime, while the fair band is a more tolerant operating range. In the inspected window, DD returns to the strict band after about $1.05$~h and to the fair band after about $1.00$~h; DM returns after about $1.17$~h and $0.25$~h, respectively; HECM returns after about $2.37$~h and $1.16$~h; and HDM does not re-enter either band. \subsection{Missing samples, irregular sampling, and burst dropout}
This block covers disturbances that affect the continuity and timing of the measurement time series rather than the amplitude of one sensor channel alone. In this context, signal integrity means that the sampled input stream remains complete, time-consistent, and updated; it does not refer only to electrical signal quality. The three cases therefore belong together: individual samples can be frozen, the sampling interval can jitter, or the input stream can be held constant over a longer dropout window.

Missing samples are the strongest global case in this block when the effect is measured by $\Delta\mathrm{MAE}$ relative to each estimator's own baseline. Under the every-50th-sample freeze, DD changes least with $\Delta\mathrm{MAE}=0.0016$, whereas DM, HDM, and HECM show larger increases of $0.0076$, $0.0079$, and $0.0029$, respectively. \section{Discussion}
\subsection{Model-specific performance behavior}
Before the individual SOC cores are interpreted, it is important to note that the online SOH support used by HDM, HECM, and DD is a shared LSTM-based neural network. The following comparison therefore separates the SOC-estimator families under a common learned health-context signal rather than comparing independently designed SOH models.

The direct-measurement model behaves as the expected drift-prone reference. Persistent current disturbances accumulate directly into the SOC estimate, and missing information cannot be corrected through an additional feedback path. HDM improves nominal calibration by adapting the effective capacity through online SOH support, but it remains structurally exposed to the same current-integration failure mode and additionally inherits the dynamics of the learned SOH assistance.

HECM benefits from interpretable parameterization, explicit voltage-residual correction, and causal observer structure. Combined with the same shared online SOH support used by the other SOH-aware variants, this explains why it is the overall robustness leader under persistent current bias and voltage noise. The same result does not mean that all noise channels are irrelevant for HECM: current noise can still affect the observer locally through current-dependent parameter scheduling and update terms. DD behaves as a compact context-rich estimator with channel-dependent strengths and weaknesses. It is especially strong under missing samples and initialization mismatch, but more exposed to voltage spikes and to propagated noise in derived inputs such as $\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}t$, $Q_c$, EFC, and the SOH context. In the present benchmark, this should be read as a design lesson of the compact deployed instance rather than as a hard class limit, because the data-driven family still retains a larger tuning space through architecture, retraining, feature selection, and state-handling choices than the more fixed-form direct or ECM-based alternatives.

\subsection{Accuracy--performance trade-offs}
A central insight of the chapter is therefore that low clean-condition MAE does not imply strong disturbed-operation performance. The trade-off is not only between accuracy and computational complexity, but also between calibration strength, correction capability, recovery speed, disturbance propagation, and the degree to which a model relies on one dominant trigger variable. The benchmark also shows that estimator comparison should not collapse everything into one leaderboard: a deployment-oriented comparison must separate nominal accuracy, disturbance robustness, recovery after inconsistency, and implementation feasibility.

\subsection{Implications for BMS deployment}
\label{sec:robustness_decision_interpretation}
If lowest nominal SOC error under clean sensing is the main design priority, HDM is the recommended class because it achieves the lowest baseline MAE of $0.0024$. The corresponding trade-off is strong sensitivity to current bias and the lack of recovery to the baseline bands in the inspected initialization-mismatch window. If the primary priority is best overall robustness under uncertain measurement quality, HECM is the strongest choice because it is most resilient under current bias, remains nearly unchanged under voltage noise, and still preserves competitive baseline accuracy; the trade-off is the larger flash footprint and slower strict-band recovery than DD. If fastest resynchronization after restart or wrong initial state dominates the design, DD is the strongest option because it shows the fastest strict-band recovery in the local initialization analysis, with the trade-off of greater sensitivity to voltage spikes and weaker performance than HECM under current bias.

If resilience to missing samples and signal freezing dominates the design, DD again becomes attractive because it shows the smallest MAE increase under missing samples and strong post-disturbance stability, while clean-condition accuracy remains below HDM and voltage-driven disturbances require a careful feature-pipeline design. If iterative development headroom and future model refinement dominate the design strategy, DD is also attractive because architecture, features, and training can be updated directly while keeping the deployment target fixed; the present compact instance is still weaker than HECM in the strongest bias- and voltage-driven stress cases, but it retains the clearest path for systematic model iteration. If the main objective is maximum implementation simplicity and the largest resource margin, DM remains the simplest class by far, but it also shows the weakest robustness under biased current measurements and missing-sample stress.

The practical message of the chapter is therefore not that one estimator wins universally. Estimator selection must instead be tied to the disturbance profile, recovery requirements, implementation constraints, and expected model-maintenance strategy of the target BMS. The footprint analysis in Section~\ref{sec:robustness_embedded_preparation} further narrows this decision space: all four deployment-prepared variants remain within the common embedded budget, persistent state memory is negligible for all classes, HECM is the tightest flash case, and DM keeps the largest implementation margin.

\subsection{Limitations and future extensions}
The current benchmark phase is based on one representative dataset trajectory and should later be extended to additional cells, broader ageing states, and cross-cell validation before stronger generalization claims are made. The spike study currently uses isolated spike events; clustered disturbances, longer spike sequences, and hardware-level disturbance injection may reveal additional model differences. The initialization test for DD remains an online-state proxy based on $Q_c$ perturbation rather than an explicit latent-state reset. This is methodologically justified, but it should still be interpreted as a class-specific approximation. The next benchmark stages should add the remaining extended scenarios, more cells, pack-level experiments, and later hardware-in-the-loop or real embedded runtime measurements while keeping the same performance-oriented evaluation logic.

\section{Conclusion}
Taken together, Chapter 6 shows that SOC-estimator quality for battery-management deployment cannot be reduced to clean-condition accuracy alone. The chapter compares representative estimator families -- direct measurement, SOH-supported direct measurement, equivalent-circuit-based hybrid estimation, and data-driven neural estimation -- under the same disturbed measurement protocol. The purpose is therefore not to declare one model universally best, but to identify which strengths and weaknesses become visible when different modelling paradigms are exposed to bias, noise, missing information, timing disturbance, spikes, and initialization mismatch.

Under the emphasized disturbance profile, HECM is the strongest overall robustness compromise because its voltage-feedback structure limits several measurement-driven error mechanisms. HDM remains the clean-condition leader, DD shows the strongest recovery and missing-sample behavior, and DM provides the simplest reference while exposing the expected drift vulnerability. This split is the main result: estimator selection must follow the expected disturbance profile, recovery requirement, and implementation context of the target BMS rather than a single ranking.

The broader implication is that practical BMS evaluation needs more than MAE or another average accuracy score. Average error remains necessary, but it must be complemented by local disturbance response, recovery behavior, tail errors, signal-continuity sensitivity, and the way disturbed measurements propagate through derived online features. The next step is therefore not another abstract class comparison, but the concrete transfer of learned state estimators into embedded execution. Chapter 7 builds on this robustness perspective and turns to recurrent neural SOC and SOH estimation on microcontrollers, where causal state handling, compression, quantization, and runtime feasibility become part of the estimator design itself.

\section{Feature Engineering and Reference Labels}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The SOC estimator consumes the six-dimensional feature vector
\[
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{SOC}}_t=\bigl[U(t),\,I(t),\,T(t),\,Q_c(t),\,\tfrac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}t}(t),\,\tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)\bigr],
\]
where the temporal derivatives are computed by centred finite differences. The SOH estimator uses a slower-varying feature stack,
\[
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{SOH}}_t=\bigl[t,\,U(t),\,I(t),\,T(t),\,\mathrm{EFC}(t),\,Q_c(t)\bigr],
\]
with cumulative time stamp $t$, equivalent full cycles $\mathrm{EFC}(t)$, and cumulative charge state $Q_c(t)$ capturing the longer degradation context.
\item All channels are robustly normalised so that the exact same scaling statistics can be embedded into the firmware. Instead of mean and variance, the chapter follows the paper in using median $m_k$ and interquartile range $r_k$,
\[
\tilde{x}_{t,k}=\frac{x_{t,k}-m_k}{r_k+\varepsilon}, \qquad \varepsilon=10^{-6},
\]
which reduces sensitivity to outliers and keeps host-side replay and MCU execution numerically aligned.\autocite{huberRobustStatistics2009,pedregosaScikitlearnMachineLearning2012}
\item The SOC and SOH reference labels are constructed from the Coulomb-counting and capacity-check-up procedures defined in Section~\ref{sec:datasets} (Equations~\ref{eq:lfp_soc_reference} and~\ref{eq:lfp_soh_reference}). Both label traces rely on information unavailable during deployment -- sustained CV-based full-charge resets and periodic full-capacity discharges -- and are used exclusively for training and evaluation. On the STM32, the estimators operate only on streamed measurements and their internal recurrent states.
\item The embedded deployment then follows a strictly stateful streaming regime: hidden and cell states are forwarded sample by sample along the full sequence without sliding input windows. Host-side replay and STM32 replay therefore use identical inference conditions. Figure~\ref{fig:embedded_lstm_schematic} summarizes this shared stateful LSTM\,+\,MLP structure that is later specialized to the SOC and SOH tasks.
\end{itemize}

\section{LSTM Architecture and Training Setup}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Both SOC and SOH estimators employ the same recurrent equation set, but with task-specific feature sets and sizes, as summarised in Figure~\ref{fig:embedded_architecture}. The SOC model uses a single-layer LSTM with hidden size $H=64$ that consumes a six-dimensional feature vector $\{U,I,T,Q_c,\mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}t\}$, followed by an MLP head with one ReLU-activated hidden layer of width 64 and a sigmoid output neuron that constrains the estimate to $[0,1]$. The SOH model uses the same structure with hidden size $H=128$ and an MLP hidden width of 128, but consumes a different six-dimensional feature vector $\{t,U,I,T,\mathrm{EFC},Q_c\}$ and ends in a purely linear output neuron because the slowly varying SOH target does not benefit from a saturating output activation. In contrast to the hourly-aggregated SOH estimator of Chapter~6, both models here operate directly on the sample-wise \SI{1}{\hertz} stream. The implementation follows the standard LSTM gate formulation in PyTorch, with propagated hidden and cell states as described in the recurrent-neural-network literature.\autocite{liptonCriticalReviewRecurrent2015,paszkePyTorchImperativeStyle2019}
\item This restriction to a single-layer LSTM\,+\,MLP architecture is deliberate. The focus is not an extensive architecture search over GRUs, Transformers, or deeper recurrent stacks, but a transparent and reproducible embedded tool flow for stateful estimators whose hidden-state handling can later be re-implemented faithfully in C. A single-layer LSTM reduces to one compact matrix-vector product per gate step, which can be written as a numerically stable stateful C kernel; sharing the same backbone for both SOC and SOH further minimises the firmware footprint. The deeper SOH estimator of Chapter~6 -- a three-layer LSTM with hidden size 192, a feature projection block, and two residual post-processing blocks -- achieves a better validation MAE (1.48\% vs.\ 2.62\% for this chapter's model), but structured pruning at any practically useful compression rate collapses that model's accuracy entirely, making the full training-to-MCU pipeline impossible. The collapse can be attributed to the interaction of depth and residual structure: in a three-layer LSTM, removing even 20\,\% of the hidden units degrades the representation passed from layer~1 to layer~2, and this degraded signal is amplified again at layer~3, so errors compound multiplicatively across the stack rather than remaining local. The two residual MLP blocks that follow the LSTM exacerbate this further, because they learn fine-grained additive corrections to the full 192-dimensional LSTM output; once the recurrent core has been compressed and its output quality has dropped, the residual corrections can no longer compensate and the prediction head receives a fundamentally degraded input. In a single-layer model with no residual post-processing, the same pruning step removes redundant units without triggering this cross-layer cascade, and the remaining units can be recovered by short finetuning. The architecture choice in this chapter is therefore not a simplification for its own sake but a direct consequence of this pruning-sensitivity finding: a model deep and complex enough to match the accuracy of the Chapter~6 estimator would, by the same token, be too rigid to support the structured compression workflow that the MCU deployment pipeline requires.
\item Both SOC and SOH models are trained with mean-squared-error loss against the corresponding reference label at each training sample, while validation additionally tracks MAE, RMSE, and $R^2$. The resulting checkpoints, configuration files, and optimizer states are archived so that all later pruning, quantization, and firmware-export steps can be replayed deterministically.
\item Training is carried out on NVIDIA A100-class GPU hardware in mixed precision (FP16). Both tasks use the AdamW optimiser, cosine-annealing warm restarts, gradient clipping, and mini-batch training.\autocite{loshchilovDecoupledWeightDecay2017,loshchilovSGDRStochasticGradient2016,micikeviciusMixedPrecisionTraining2017,pascanuDifficultyTrainingRecurrent2012}
\item For SOC, the learning rate is $1.5\times10^{-4}$, the batch size is 512, and the sequence chunk length is 2024. For SOH, the learning rate is $2.0\times10^{-4}$, the batch size is 128, and the chunk length is 2048.
\item Although deployment is fully stateful and step-wise, training uses truncated backpropagation through time over these chunks. Within each chunk, the hidden and cell states are propagated exactly as in deployment, so the stateful update mechanism is already learned during optimisation while the chunking still enables randomized sampling, faster convergence, and better generalization across the diverse ageing trajectories.
\item The base models are first evaluated in streaming mode on the full ageing trajectory of a representative LFP cell and serve as the numerical reference for all subsequent experiments. These same checkpoints are then pruned, quantized, and exported to STM32 firmware, where identical feature streams are replayed so that accuracy, flash, RAM, latency, and energy always refer to the same stateful sequence. Figure~\ref{fig:embedded_pipeline_original} summarizes this end-to-end path from training to embedded benchmarking.
\end{itemize}

\section{Structured Pruning for MCU Deployment}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Pruning is applied as structured magnitude pruning at the level of complete LSTM hidden units, because whole-channel removal is far better suited to efficient C implementations on a microcontroller than unstructured sparse masks.\autocite{hanLearningBothWeights2015,blalockWhatStateNeural2020}
\item The pruning score aggregates the L2 norms of the incoming weights for each hidden unit across all four LSTM gates,
\[
s_h=\sum_{g\in\{i,f,o,g\}}\left(\lVert W_{\mathrm{ih}}^{(g)}[h,:]\rVert_2+\lVert W_{\mathrm{hh}}^{(g)}[h,:]\rVert_2\right),
\]
so that hidden units with the smallest aggregated saliency are removed first. This follows the same structured-sparsity intuition used in recurrent-network pruning literature.\autocite{wenLearningStructuredSparsity2016,lobachevaStructuredSparsificationGated2020,narangExploringSparsityRecurrent2017}
\item In the final deployment, the SOC estimator is reduced from 64 to 45 hidden units and the SOH estimator from 128 to 90 hidden units. In both cases this corresponds to roughly 30\% channel-wise sparsity.
\item Rows and columns are removed jointly from the recurrent gate matrices and the downstream MLP weights so that the pruned network remains a valid stateful recurrent mapping.
\item After each pruning step the model is fine-tuned again. The chapter therefore treats pruning not as a single static compression event, but as a repeated prune-and-recover cycle that progressively reduces recurrent state size while preserving the learned temporal mapping.
\item The engineering purpose of pruning is direct: reduce recurrent state size, shrink flash and RAM demand, and shorten on-device inference time without materially compromising streaming accuracy. Figure~\ref{fig:embedded_pruning_schematic} illustrates this channel-wise removal process at the level of hidden units and associated MLP rows.
\end{itemize}

\section{INT8 Quantization and Manual STM32 Export}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Quantization complements pruning by reducing the numerical precision of the remaining weights while leaving the topology unchanged. In this chapter, the recurrent weights are stored as signed INT8 values with associated floating-point scales so that the firmware can retain a transparent mapping back to the PyTorch reference.\autocite{jacobQuantizationTrainingNeural2017,krishnamoorthiQuantizingDeepConvolutional2018} This aligns with the broader observation from recent pruning/quantization benchmarks that aggressive compression can preserve useful accuracy when the deployment path is engineered carefully.\autocite{schulzePQBenchBenchmarking2025}
\item The implementation uses post-training per-row symmetric INT8 quantization for the input-to-gate and hidden-to-gate matrices of the LSTM. For each row, the scale is chosen as
\[
s=\frac{\max_h |w_h|}{127},
\]
so that the maximum absolute weight maps close to the signed INT8 limits $\{-128,\ldots,127\}$ while zero remains exactly representable.
\item Biases and activations remain in FP32. This design is pragmatic: it preserves numerical stability, keeps the firmware implementation close to the floating-point reference, and still reduces recurrent weight storage by roughly a factor of four relative to FP32 storage.
\item During inference, the embedded gate computation reconstructs the effective weights from INT8 codes and FP32 scales. A built-in streaming comparison validates this conversion and typically yields MAE differences below $10^{-4}$ between exported and reference implementations, which indicates that the recurrent dynamics are preserved numerically.
\item The exported model parameters are written as C headers and integrated into manually implemented stateful LSTM\,+\,MLP kernels. This manual route is chosen because common export toolchains such as STM32Cube.AI do not natively support the continuous hidden-state handling required for these estimators.\autocite{ONNXRuntimeHighperformance,NVIDIATensorRTHighPerformance,XCUBEAISTM32CubeExpansion}
\item The result is a fully transparent firmware path: every scaling constant, every recurrent state update, and every arithmetic step can be audited and reproduced outside the proprietary toolchain. Figure~\ref{fig:embedded_quantization_schematic} summarizes the FP32-to-INT8 conversion, scale handling, and STM32 export flow.
\end{itemize}

\section{Benchmark Methodology and KPI Logic}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The benchmark is executed on an STM32 Nucleo-144 board with an STM32H753ZI microcontroller, providing \SI{2}{\mega\byte} flash and \SI{1}{\mega\byte} SRAM, which together define the available code, weight, and runtime-buffer budget for the deployed estimators.\autocite{STM32Nucleo144Boards2025_Datasheet,naguibMicroprocessorExecutionTime2022}
\item Starting from the trained LSTM\,+\,MLP checkpoints, three embedded variants are evaluated for each task: the dense FP32 baseline, the structured-pruned FP32 model, and the quantized INT8-weight deployment. For all of them, identical feature streams from the LFP dataset are replayed both on the host and on the STM32 so that accuracy, latency, and resource measurements always refer to the same stateful input sequence.
\item The host-side replay operates directly on the reference PyTorch implementation and produces MAE, RMSE, $R^2$, error histograms, parity-style comparisons, and long-horizon trajectory views. These simulations serve as the high-precision numerical reference against which the embedded deployments are later judged.
\item The embedded benchmark injects the same feature vectors via UART into dedicated SOC and SOH firmware images, measures end-to-end host latency, measures pure on-device inference time through the DWT cycle counter, and derives energy per inference from the average inference time and an empirically calibrated board power of \SI{0.5}{\watt}.
\item RAM is computed as the sum of statically allocated `.data` and `.bss` sections plus the maximum observed stack usage during streaming. Flash is taken from the linker output of the STM32CubeIDE projects. Tables~\ref{tab:embedded_soc_kpi} and~\ref{tab:embedded_soh_kpi} therefore summarize specifically the STM32 resource and timing indicators, whereas the trajectory and distribution metrics remain in the host-side streaming analysis.
\item The chapter distinguishes carefully between communication-dominated system latency and pure on-device inference time. This distinction matters because UART framing and host scheduling can dominate the end-to-end delay, although the actual neural computation remains well within the real-time budget of the target platform.
\end{itemize}

\section{Streaming Prediction Accuracy}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The results section starts with full-sequence streaming replay over the complete ageing trajectory. Figures~\ref{fig:embedded_soc_dashboard} and~\ref{fig:embedded_soh_dashboard} therefore provide the central long-horizon view of how the dense, pruned, and quantized deployments behave under identical stateful replay conditions.
\item For SOC, the tracking remains tight across the entire dynamic range. The residuals stay centered around zero, and the quantized model overlaps the full-precision baseline so closely that the two traces are often visually almost indistinguishable. This indicates that the per-row symmetric quantization preserves the recurrent dynamics sufficiently well for accurate long-horizon SOC tracking.
\item The pruned SOC model also remains highly accurate, but shows slightly stronger smoothing in highly dynamic regions. In the interpretation of the paper, this is the expected consequence of the reduced hidden-state dimensionality after structured channel removal.
\item For SOH, the task is structurally harder because the estimator has to infer slow degradation from sparse check-up events and continuous monitoring data. The paper therefore removes the discrete capacity tests from the estimator input and retains only the remaining check-up blocks and operating segments, so that the estimator must infer the degradation trend from causally available evidence only.
\item A causal smoothing stage is applied to the raw SOH predictions. Specifically, the chapter follows the paper in using an exponential moving average with smoothing factor $\alpha=10^{-6}$ together with a downward-rate limiter of $2\times10^{-8}$ per step in order to suppress unphysical jumps while maintaining a monotonic long-term degradation trend.
\item After this filtering, the pruned and quantized SOH models still track the dense baseline with similar fidelity and preserve the long-term trend despite visible differences in small-scale variability. Across the embedded variants, the resulting full-stream accuracy remains in the same overall range already noted earlier: about \SIrange{2.3}{2.8}{\percent} MAE for SOC and about \SIrange{0.9}{1.5}{\percent} MAE for SOH, both reported relative to full scale.
\end{itemize}

\section{Error Distribution and Local Transient Behaviour}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Figures~\ref{fig:embedded_soc_error} and~\ref{fig:embedded_soh_error} analyse the statistical distribution of the prediction errors. For SOC, the distribution is narrow and symmetric, which indicates the absence of systematic bias across the full streaming replay.
\item The SOC boxplots further show that the pruned model achieves the smallest median MAE and the narrowest interquartile range. In other words, pruning does not merely reduce resource demand for the SOC task, but acts in this case like a mild regularizer that slightly improves the dense baseline.
\item For SOH, the error distribution is broader, which is consistent with the greater intrinsic difficulty of estimating capacity fade from operational data. Even so, the bulk of the SOH errors remains within a practically usable tolerance band of approximately $\pm 2\%$, which keeps the embedded estimator relevant for onboard diagnostics despite the more difficult regression target.
\item Global metrics alone would still hide local failures. The chapter therefore also zooms into a high-dynamic pulse segment between 30k and 40k seconds (Figure~\ref{fig:embedded_soc_zoom_pulse}) and into a representative check-up sequence between 657k and 897k seconds (Figure~\ref{fig:embedded_soc_zoom_checkup}).

\item In the check-up phase, all models converge well and remain stable over long integration horizons. This is an important result for the embedded formulation because it shows that the strictly stateful streaming execution does not accumulate visible drift even across long full-charge and full-discharge sequences.
\end{itemize}

\section{Resource Efficiency, Latency, and Trade-Off Summary}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The KPI tables already show that pruning and quantization affect SOC and SOH differently, but Figures~\ref{fig:embedded_model_sizes_original} and~\ref{fig:embedded_latency_hist} condense the same trade-off visually across model size, memory, and timing.
\item For each deployment, the model complexity can first be estimated from the parameter count under idealized storage assumptions, namely four bytes per FP32 weight and one byte per INT8 weight. The measured flash footprint is then taken from the STM32 linker output and is systematically larger because it also includes code, constant tables, and runtime support. This gap is particularly visible for the larger SOH firmware.
\item The RAM view in Figure~\ref{fig:embedded_model_sizes_original} is based on the measured static data sections plus worst-case stack usage during streaming. It therefore approximates the true runtime requirement of the deployed kernels more realistically than a pure parameter-memory count.
\item For SOC, pruning reduces flash from \SI{105.32}{\kilo\byte} to \SI{62.27}{\kilo\byte}, reduces inference time from \SI{1.40}{\milli\second} to \SI{0.80}{\milli\second}, and reduces energy per inference from \SI{0.70}{\milli\joule} to \SI{0.40}{\milli\joule}. This corresponds to gains of roughly 41--43\%.
\item Quantization reduces the SOC flash footprint further to \SI{52.48}{\kilo\byte}, but increases pure inference time to \SI{6.99}{\milli\second} and energy per inference to \SI{3.49}{\milli\joule} because the software kernel has to reconstruct FP32 values from INT8 codes and scales.
\item For SOH, pruning reduces flash from \SI{335.00}{\kilo\byte} to \SI{182.41}{\kilo\byte}, reduces inference time from \SI{22.73}{\milli\second} to \SI{12.72}{\milli\second}, and reduces energy per inference from \SI{11.37}{\milli\joule} to \SI{6.36}{\milli\joule}. Quantization reduces flash further to \SI{138.00}{\kilo\byte}, but raises inference time and energy to \SI{29.21}{\milli\second} and \SI{14.60}{\milli\joule}.
\item The latency distribution in Figure~\ref{fig:embedded_latency_hist} reveals the same hierarchy. On-device SOC inference remains in the sub-\SI{10}{\milli\second} regime even for the quantized kernel, whereas SOH updates remain in the approximate range of \SIrange{12}{30}{\milli\second}. Including UART overhead still leaves end-to-end latencies below roughly \SI{20}{\milli\second} for SOC and below roughly \SI{50}{\milli\second} for SOH, which is comfortably inside typical stationary-storage BMS timing limits.\autocite{plettBatteryManagementSystems2015,berecibarCriticalReviewState2016}
\item The model-size and latency plots therefore condense a clear pattern: quantization is the strongest lever for non-volatile memory, while pruning is the strongest lever for energy and inference time on the current hand-written STM32 implementation.
\item To summarize this multi-objective trade-off more compactly, the chapter uses the composite utility score
\[
U_v=\frac{1}{4}\left(
\frac{\mathrm{MAE}_v}{\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{Base}}}+
\frac{F_v}{F_{\mathrm{Base}}}+
\frac{R_v}{R_{\mathrm{Base}}}+
\frac{E_v}{E_{\mathrm{Base}}}
\right),
\]
where $\mathrm{MAE}$ denotes the streaming mean absolute error, $F$ the measured flash footprint, $R$ the measured total RAM usage, and $E$ the measured energy per inference. By construction, the dense baseline has $U=1$, and values below one indicate an average improvement across these four objectives.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implications for MCU-Based BMS}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The central engineering outcome of the chapter is that all investigated deployments satisfy the timing constraints of typical stationary-storage BMS applications with comfortable margin.
\item Even when communication overhead is included, end-to-end SOC latencies remain below roughly \SI{20}{\milli\second} and SOH latencies below roughly \SI{50}{\milli\second}. The pure compute time on the MCU stays below \SI{1.5}{\milli\second} for dense and pruned SOC and within roughly \SIrange{12}{30}{\milli\second} for the SOH variants.
\item The chapter also makes clear that there is no universally best compression strategy. On the current STM32 implementation, pruning provides the most balanced operating point because it reduces flash, RAM, inference time, and energy simultaneously, whereas quantization is attractive mainly when flash capacity is the dominating constraint and additional runtime overhead remains acceptable.
\item This conclusion is reflected directly in the utility score: for SOC, the pruned variant reaches $U=0.71$ and is therefore the most favorable trade-off, whereas the quantized SOC deployment reaches $U=1.83$ because the INT8 kernel increases energy strongly. For SOH, both compressed variants remain close to the baseline with $U\approx0.9$--$1.0$, which means that flash savings and accuracy degradation roughly balance each other in the aggregate score.
\item From the perspective of the dissertation, this chapter provides the concrete bridge between data-driven estimator design and the actual embedded controller. It shows that SOC and SOH estimators can be co-located on one STM32H7-class platform, but that architecture, compression, firmware implementation, and task-specific accuracy requirements must be selected jointly rather than sequentially.
\item At the same time, the chapter also exposes the limits of the present study. The experiments are based on one rich LFP campaign, one cell chemistry, and one embedded platform, and they do not yet include systematic measurement-noise, sensor-drift, or fault-injection experiments on the microcontroller itself. Future work should therefore extend the analysis to additional chemistries, temperature ranges, loading regimes, and pack-level BMS integration, and should compare the present LSTM backbone against alternative recurrent or hybrid estimators such as GRUs, Transformer-style models, or neural-augmented equivalent-circuit observers.\autocite{liptonCriticalReviewRecurrent2015,singhDeepMachineLearning2024,gaoEnhancedStateofchargeEstimation2022,wangFusionEstimationLithiumion2022}
\item This naturally motivates the next chapter. Once Chapter~7 has established that pruning and quantization can change flash, latency, energy, and even accuracy in architecture-specific ways, Chapter~8 examines these compression effects explicitly as an object of study rather than only as one deployment step in a single embedded pipeline.
\end{itemize}
\chapter{Architecture-Dependent Effects of Pruning and Quantization}
\section{Problem Statement}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The embedded deployment study already demonstrates that pruning and quantization can improve the feasibility of MCU-based inference.
\item The next scientific step is to determine how strongly these gains depend on the underlying estimator architecture.
\item This question is important because compression methods that work well for one model family may degrade another model family disproportionately in terms of accuracy, numerical stability, or execution behaviour.
\item A cross-architecture analysis therefore extends the embedded perspective from a single deployment pipeline toward a broader model-compression design framework.
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodological Strengths and Limitations Across the Studies}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item A key strength of the dissertation is that it spans several layers of evidence: controlled ageing data, structured disturbance benchmarks, measured embedded deployment metrics, and the BMS-STEP hardware platform for practical validation.
\item Another strength is the common engineering perspective across otherwise different studies. Ageing estimation, robustness benchmarking, and microcontroller deployment are treated as connected parts of one BMS-related research problem.
\item At the same time, the studies rely on different datasets, task definitions, and evaluation settings. Their numerical results are therefore not interchangeable in a strict one-to-one sense, but must be interpreted as complementary evidence for the same overarching design problem.
\item The data-driven chapters remain dependent on the breadth and quality of the available datasets. Atypical degradation trajectories, incomplete measurements, and limited coverage of rare operating conditions continue to restrict the generality of the reported results.
\item The embedded and hardware-related chapters are likewise shaped by the selected microcontroller targets, firmware environment, and laboratory setup. The transferability of the exact resource figures to other platforms must therefore be interpreted with appropriate engineering caution.
\end{itemize}

\section{Positioning Within the State of Research}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item A large part of the existing literature concentrates on one dimension at a time, for example on estimation accuracy, on a particular neural architecture, or on a single deployment toolchain.
\item The present dissertation contributes a broader view by showing that battery AI methods must be assessed across model quality, disturbance sensitivity, embedded feasibility, and system integration at the same time.
\item In this sense, the work is positioned between algorithm development and embedded systems engineering. It neither treats the battery estimator as a purely offline machine-learning problem nor reduces the hardware side to an implementation appendix.
\item This placement is especially important for battery-management applications, because the practical usefulness of an estimator depends on the full chain from measurement acquisition and signal quality to firmware integration and on-device inference behaviour.
\end{itemize}

\section{Technical Implications for AI-Based BMS}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The combined results imply that estimator selection for a BMS should begin with the target use case and the expected sensing conditions rather than with benchmark accuracy under ideal signals alone.
\item Robustness properties must be treated as a design criterion before and not after embedded compression, because pruning or quantization of a disturbance-sensitive model can further reduce its practical value.
\item The hardware chapter additionally shows that an adaptable research platform is not a secondary convenience, but an enabling condition for meaningful embedded-AI experimentation. The ability to exchange estimator methods, separate BMS logic from state-estimation logic, and validate new models in a controlled firmware environment is methodologically important.
\item For engineering practice, this means that AI-based battery estimation should be developed together with the execution environment, measurement chain, and integration path instead of being optimized only in an offline data-analysis setting.
\end{itemize}

\section{Integrated Design Guidelines}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Estimators should not be selected by clean-condition accuracy alone, because disturbance sensitivity and recovery behaviour strongly affect practical usefulness.
\item Robustness analysis should precede aggressive compression, since a highly compressed estimator with poor disturbance tolerance offers limited deployment value.
\item Quantization and pruning must be treated as architecture-dependent design choices rather than as universally transferable post-processing steps.
\item Dataset quality, estimator quality, and embedded execution quality should be analysed separately so that improvements in one domain are not mistaken for improvements in another.
\item Algorithmic claims gain practical relevance only when they are connected to a credible validation chain that includes sensing, firmware execution, and hardware integration.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Conclusion and Outlook}
\section{Overall Conclusion}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The work shows that deployment-oriented battery AI emerges only when model design, robustness, embedded execution, and hardware integration are treated as one linked problem.
\item Neural architectures enable powerful battery state estimation and ageing estimation, but their practical value depends on how they behave under disturbances and how efficiently they can be transferred to embedded hardware.
\item Robustness benchmarking reveals that estimator quality changes fundamentally once measurement disturbances, timing irregularities, and initialization mismatch are considered explicitly.
\item Embedded implementation demonstrates that compression, latency, and memory demand are not secondary engineering details, but core evaluation dimensions for practical battery-management applications.
\item The combination of algorithmic studies and custom hardware integration therefore leads to a unified framework for analysing battery intelligence across performance, hardware, and deployment requirements.
\end{itemize}

\section{Boundary Conditions and Remaining Gaps}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The reported findings must be interpreted in the context of the available datasets, the selected estimator classes, and the concrete hardware environments used throughout the work.
\item The general design logic of the dissertation is transferable, but the exact quantitative trade-offs between accuracy, robustness, memory demand, and latency can shift when other cell chemistries, sensing chains, control architectures, or MCU families are considered.
\item Further validation across broader datasets, longer ageing horizons, and additional embedded targets is therefore necessary to consolidate the transferability of the derived design rules.
\end{itemize}

\section{Outlook}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Future work extends the embedded analysis by comparing pruning and quantization across a wider range of model architectures.
\item Further experiments on the BMS-STEP platform can deepen the relation between estimator design, signal quality, and practical system behaviour under laboratory and hardware-in-the-loop conditions.
\item Long-duration cycling and additional datasets can refine the calibration and validation of SoC and SoH methods across broader ageing states and operating conditions.
\item The longer-term objective is the transfer of deployment-oriented battery AI from controlled laboratory systems toward scalable and reproducible BMS integration in larger energy-storage applications.
\end{itemize}
