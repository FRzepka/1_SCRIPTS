\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}
This dissertation investigates deployment-oriented artificial intelligence for battery management systems and combines model development, robustness analysis, embedded implementation, and hardware integration within one common research framework.

\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
This dissertation develops a deployment-oriented perspective on artificial intelligence for battery management systems. The work begins with neural-network-based ageing estimation for stationary storage systems, continues with robustness benchmarking of embedded battery state estimation under disturbed measurements, and then addresses the transfer of SOC and SOH models to microcontrollers using pruning and quantization. A comparative analysis across model architectures extends the compression perspective, while a dedicated hardware chapter documents the custom battery management system platform and the practical route from algorithm development to embedded execution. The central result is that battery intelligence must be evaluated not only by nominal prediction accuracy, but against a combined set of battery-management requirements across performance, hardware, and deployment.

\chapter*{Executive Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Executive Summary}
This dissertation is structured as a continuous, application-oriented account of artificial intelligence in battery management. Its main focus lies on neural architectures for ageing estimation in stationary storage systems, the robustness of embedded state estimation under realistic measurement disturbances, the efficient execution of such models on microcontrollers through pruning and quantization, and their integration into a real hardware and BMS platform. The overarching idea is that AI-enabled BMS methods should be evaluated not only by accuracy, but along the three requirement dimensions of performance, hardware, and deployment.

\chapter{Introduction and Motivation}
\chapterrole{}

Battery management systems require reliable internal-state information to operate lithium-ion batteries safely, efficiently, and over long service intervals. Estimates of state of charge and state of health influence charging control, power limitation, lifetime-aware operation, and higher-level energy-management decisions. At the same time, battery behaviour becomes increasingly nonlinear once ageing, operating-point variation, and real measurement imperfections are taken into account. This creates a strong motivation for AI-based estimation methods that can capture complex relations beyond compact analytical descriptions.

Practical BMS deployment, however, imposes requirements that go beyond nominal prediction accuracy under controlled evaluation settings. Estimators must remain useful under disturbed measurements, limited computation and memory, causal execution, and real firmware and sensing constraints. These requirements become especially relevant in decentralized stationary-storage systems and remote microgrid-like applications, where battery supervision must remain locally available over long maintenance intervals and without permanent cloud or internet connectivity. The central scientific gap addressed in this dissertation is therefore not the absence of accurate battery-AI models as such, but the limited availability of a consistent evaluation and development framework that brings model quality, robustness, embedded feasibility, and hardware-level realization together.

\section{Research Objective and Requirement Dimensions}
The research objective of the dissertation is to develop and validate deployment-oriented neural-network methods for battery management systems along three linked requirement dimensions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The first requirement dimension addresses estimator performance. This includes not only nominal accuracy under controlled conditions, but also robustness against disturbed measurements, recovery behaviour after state inconsistency, and stable operation under realistic signal corruption and imperfect online conditions.
\item The second dimension concerns hardware requirements. Battery-AI methods intended for practical BMS use must be judged against the limits of the target platform, including memory demand, runtime latency, integration constraints, and restrictions imposed by the measurement chain itself.
\item The third dimension focuses on deployment requirements from model concept to embedded realization. This includes feature engineering, architecture selection, compression, model-to-device transfer, adaptability, scalability, and the firmware-level steps required to turn a trained estimator into a deployable BMS component.
\end{itemize}
These three dimensions are treated as interdependent rather than separate evaluation layers. The central argument of the dissertation is that battery-AI methods become practically meaningful only when predictive quality, embedded realizability, and hardware-level integration are developed and assessed together.

\section{Scientific Contributions}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item At the conceptual level, the dissertation establishes a unified deployment-oriented framework for evaluating battery-AI methods across performance, hardware, and deployment requirements. This framework provides the common perspective through which the subsequent studies are interpreted rather than treating accuracy, robustness, and embedded realization as isolated concerns.
\item For ageing estimation in stationary-storage cells, the work introduces cumulative-current features and temporal lag construction as a compact way of representing ageing-relevant information. In the first study, this representation is examined with an MLP-based baseline; within the broader dissertation context, however, it serves mainly as the starting point of a larger development path that later extends toward recurrent architectures and deployment-oriented estimator design.
\item A further methodological contribution is the robustness benchmark for embedded battery state estimation. Here, direct-measurement, hybrid, and data-driven estimator classes are compared under controlled disturbances such as bias, noise, missing samples, timing irregularities, and initialization mismatch, so that estimator quality is assessed under deployment-relevant rather than purely nominal conditions.
\item On the implementation side, the dissertation analyses how SOC and SOH estimators can be transferred to microcontrollers through pruning, quantization, and deployment-oriented STM32 benchmarking. This part of the work links model design to concrete trade-offs in memory demand, runtime latency, and practical firmware integration.
\item The experimental basis of the dissertation is provided by the Battery Management System State-Estimation Test Platform (BMS-STEP), a custom hardware environment for repeatable embedded-AI studies within one common BMS architecture. This platform creates the technical basis for comparing interchangeable estimation methods under controlled and reproducible hardware conditions.
\item Taken together, these elements form a cumulative design perspective for deployment-oriented battery intelligence that connects representation, robustness, embedded optimization, and hardware integration. The overall contribution therefore lies not only in the individual studies, but also in their integration into one coherent development and evaluation logic.
\end{itemize}

\section{Specific Research Objectives}
From this overall framing, the dissertation pursues five specific research objectives:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item To identify how neural-network methods can represent battery ageing with sufficient practical utility for stationary-storage applications and long-term condition monitoring.
\item To assess under which disturbance conditions embedded battery state estimators preserve acceptable accuracy, convergence behaviour, and recovery capability.
\item To evaluate how AI-based SOC and SOH estimators can be transferred to microcontrollers through resource-aware pruning, quantization, and model-to-device adaptation.
\item To analyse under which architectural conditions pruning and quantization remain effective without disproportionately degrading predictive performance.
\item To define how a hardware platform and validation chain should be structured in order to support repeatable, hardware-level evaluation of AI-based BMS methods under realistic embedded conditions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Structure of the Dissertation and Development Path}
The dissertation follows a cumulative structure in which study-specific methods, results, and local interpretation remain within the contribution chapters, while the common technical basis and the cross-study implications are developed in the surrounding chapters.

The progression of the work is intentionally staged. The ageing-estimation chapter first addresses how battery-relevant information can be represented in a neural estimator. The robustness benchmark then shifts the focus from nominal performance to disturbance-aware evaluation under realistic measurement corruption. The embedded-AI chapter extends this perspective toward microcontroller deployment, where compression, memory, and latency become decisive design variables. The subsequent compression chapter generalizes this question across architectures, while the hardware chapter and the BMS-STEP platform provide the common laboratory basis on which these deployment questions can be tested under realistic embedded conditions.

The shared laboratory hardware basis of the dissertation is the Battery Management System State-Estimation Test Platform (BMS-STEP), a custom 4s research BMS developed to compare and exchange embedded state-estimation methods on one reproducible system basis. The final synthesis chapter integrates the findings across all studies and derives the cumulative contribution of the work to deployment-oriented battery intelligence.

\chapter{Battery-Management Requirement Framework}

Battery-management systems require estimation methods that satisfy several conditions simultaneously. In practical operation, a model must provide accurate and stable state information, remain robust under realistic measurement imperfections, fit within the available embedded resources, and be transferable into an executable BMS implementation. For this reason, the present work organizes the relevant battery-management requirements into three connected areas: performance requirements, hardware requirements, and deployment requirements. This framework provides the common basis on which the individual studies of the dissertation are interpreted. Each study emphasizes different requirement groups, but all of them contribute to the same overarching question of how neural-network methods can be developed for deployment-oriented battery management.

\section{Performance Requirements}
Performance requirements describe the estimation quality that a BMS must obtain from an AI-based method during operation. In this dissertation, the central criteria are accuracy, robustness, and convergence behaviour. Accuracy is required because the estimator must provide reliable state information under nominal conditions. Robustness becomes essential once the input signals are affected by bias, noise, missing samples, or timing irregularities. Convergence behaviour determines whether the estimator can recover in a controlled manner after initialization mismatch, signal corruption, or temporary state inconsistency. These criteria are examined most directly in the robustness benchmark, but they also remain relevant when the results of the ageing-estimation and embedded-AI studies are interpreted in a broader deployment context.

\section{Hardware Requirements}
Hardware requirements arise because BMS estimators are executed on resource-limited embedded platforms rather than in unrestricted desktop environments. The most important quantities considered in this work are RAM usage, Flash usage, and inference time. These metrics determine whether a model can be executed on the target controller with sufficient timing margin and without exhausting the available memory. In addition, hardware-related conditions such as measurement quality, controller structure, and system integration influence which estimator concepts remain feasible in practice. These questions become particularly visible in the embedded-AI chapter, where memory footprint and latency are quantified directly, and in the hardware chapter, which provides the BMS-oriented environment in which such constraints can be assessed experimentally.

\section{Deployment Requirements}
Deployment requirements describe how readily a model can be transferred from research development into practical BMS use. In this dissertation, the most relevant aspects are model complexity, adaptability, and scalability. Model complexity influences both the effort of implementation and the feasibility of later compression and hardware transfer. Adaptability refers to the extent to which modelling concepts, feature constructions, and estimator structures can be reused or modified for related battery-state estimation tasks. Scalability concerns whether a method and its optimization strategy remain viable across different model classes and target platforms. This requirement area therefore connects feature design, architecture choice, pruning, quantization, and firmware transfer within one common perspective.

\section{Coverage of the Framework in the Dissertation}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The ageing-estimation study addresses primarily the performance and deployment sides of the framework. Its focus lies on how ageing-relevant battery information can be represented in a neural estimator and how this representation can be translated into useful prediction quality for stationary-storage data.
\item The robustness benchmark concentrates most strongly on performance requirements. It shows that estimator quality in battery management cannot be reduced to nominal accuracy alone, but must also include robustness against disturbed measurements and convergence behaviour after inconsistencies.
\item The embedded-AI study connects deployment requirements with hardware requirements. In this part of the dissertation, model transfer, pruning, quantization, RAM usage, Flash usage, and inference latency become central because they determine whether a method can actually be executed on a microcontroller target.
\item The hardware chapter provides the experimental and system-level basis for this framework. It establishes a BMS-oriented platform on which different estimation methods can be integrated, exchanged, and tested under reproducible embedded conditions.
\item The architecture-dependent compression study extends the deployment perspective toward more general design rules. It examines how strongly pruning and quantization depend on model class and how scalable such optimization strategies remain across different architectures.
\end{itemize}

Taken together, these studies cover complementary parts of one common battery-management requirement framework. The framework therefore functions as the conceptual link between the chapters and as the basis for the final synthesis of the dissertation.

\chapter{State of the Art and Technical Background}

\section{Battery Management Systems}
Battery management systems supervise cell voltages, current, temperature, balancing, protection paths, and communication with higher-level control systems. Within this functional structure, SOC and SOH are not isolated diagnostic variables, but central quantities for power limitation, charging control, lifetime-aware operation, and system-level energy management.

\section{Neural Networks for Battery Ageing and State Estimation}
Neural methods are attractive in battery applications because they can represent nonlinear signal relations, exploit temporal context, and combine measured and derived variables in a data-driven manner. At the same time, their practical value depends not only on predictive accuracy, but also on interpretability, training stability, robustness, and deployment effort.

\section{Embedded AI in Battery Applications}
Embedded AI in battery applications shifts the focus from model quality alone toward deployment quality. Relevant criteria therefore include not only prediction error, but also Flash demand, RAM demand, latency, numerical stability, reproducibility, and the effort required to transfer a model from high-level research code to microcontroller inference.

\section{Compression and Model-Hardware Co-Design}
Compression and model-hardware co-design become essential as soon as AI is treated as a practical BMS component. Pruning and quantization are therefore not secondary optimizations, but central design choices that influence memory footprint, runtime behaviour, numerical precision, and ultimately the feasibility of embedded deployment.

\chapter{Experimental Basis and Hardware Ecosystem}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The experimental basis of this work combines laboratory battery datasets, embedded execution targets, and the custom BMS-STEP hardware platform for battery management.
\item This combined setup makes it possible to study battery-related artificial intelligence not only as an offline modelling problem, but also as an embedded systems problem shaped by sensing, communication, firmware partitioning, and hardware constraints.
\item The resulting experimental ecosystem forms the common basis for the ageing-estimation work, the robustness benchmark, and the embedded implementation studies.
\end{itemize}

\section{Data Sources and Ageing Campaigns}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The data basis of this work consists of two complementary domains. The first domain comprises ageing-oriented battery datasets used for the stationary-storage SOH study, whereas the second domain comprises laboratory datasets recorded in the context of the MG-FARM project and used for embedded SOC/SOH estimation and robustness analysis.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a,lfpDoeCycleAgingDataset2025}
\item Across these datasets, the core measured quantities are voltage, current, temperature, and time. From these measured signals, additional reference quantities such as transferred charge, capacity, state of health, and cumulative charge state are derived for model development and evaluation.
\item The laboratory campaigns were structured around controlled operating conditions and ageing states so that model behaviour could be analysed under reproducible charge and discharge trajectories instead of under loosely documented use cases.
\item This separation between raw measurements and derived reference targets is important because it preserves a clear distinction between physically observed signals and offline evaluation quantities generated during preprocessing.
\item The resulting data basis supports both long-term ageing analysis and deployment-oriented state-estimation studies on embedded hardware.
\end{itemize}

\section{Embedded Targets and Execution Chain}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The embedded work relies on two complementary targets: the final BMS-STEP hardware and an STM32H755-based evaluation-board setup for rapid firmware iteration and controlled implementation tests.
\item The integrated BMS hardware is used whenever sensing, balancing, protection, communication, telemetry, and on-board state estimation must be validated as one coupled system rather than as isolated software modules.
\item The evaluation-board setup is used whenever estimator code, toolchain details, memory demand, and runtime behaviour have to be checked quickly before transfer to the full BMS-STEP platform.
\item In both cases, the deployment path follows the same sequence described in the reports: state-estimation methods are first prototyped in Python or MATLAB, then rewritten as plain C modules, compiled into the STM32 firmware, and executed locally on the target hardware instead of off-board.
\item During validation, the firmware records voltage, current, temperature, and relevant intermediate estimator variables. After each run, these traces are aligned with higher-fidelity offline reference calculations for SoC and SoH on a host PC. D7.11 explicitly states that no hardware-in-the-loop setup is used in this workflow.
\item This split between evaluation-board work and integrated BMS operation is important for the dissertation because it separates pure implementation questions from questions of sensing, protection, and full system integration.
\end{itemize}

\section{Custom BMS-STEP Platform}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The custom laboratory hardware developed in this work is referred to as the Battery Management System State-Estimation Test Platform, abbreviated as BMS-STEP.
\item BMS-STEP is a physically realised 4s laboratory BMS designed to monitor and protect the cells, execute on-board SoC and SoH estimation, perform balancing, and provide the technical basis for controlled experiments with embedded battery intelligence.
\item A central reason for the in-house design is adaptability. In contrast to a commercial off-the-shelf BMS, the BMS-STEP platform gives direct access to the sensing chain, firmware structure, interfaces, and estimator integration, so that state-estimation methods can be exchanged, modified, and benchmarked repeatedly on the same hardware basis.
\item The board should therefore be understood less as a finished product and more as a configurable research platform that was built deliberately to support rapid iteration, customisation, and comparative testing.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{System Objective and Laboratory Scope}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The present implementation is a compact and reproducible 4s laboratory BMS for battery ageing studies and embedded state-estimation experiments at TU Berlin EET.
\item The platform is intended for ageing tests, cyclic and calendar experiments, and representative charging and discharging profiles rather than unrestricted field deployment in large battery racks.
\item D7.11 makes clear that exact field loads are not reproduced one-to-one in the laboratory. Instead, they are approximated within a controlled setup that allows calibration, diagnostics, repeated validation, and structured dataset generation.
\item This laboratory orientation is deliberate because it enables benchmark-style comparison of different state-estimation approaches under the same measurement chain, including classical model-based observers and modern data-driven or neural methods executed directly on the STM32 controller.
\item The platform concept is explicitly geared toward experimentation: one hardware base should support multiple estimator variants without rebuilding the full BMS around each method.
\item Validation campaigns reported in D7.11 include duty cycles derived from decentralized stationary-storage use cases with limited maintenance access. These tests were used to record voltage drift, current ripple, temperature gradients, and embedded estimator outputs under realistic operating conditions.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hardware Architecture}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The BMS-STEP platform can be understood as three tightly coupled hardware domains: the TLE9012DQU-based sensing and balancing stage, the current-measurement and protection interface, and the controller/communication island around the dual-core STM32H755.
\item The board layout keeps the high-current zone, shunt, and relay path physically separated from the low-voltage controller and communication domain. This separation matters not only for safety, but also for electromagnetic compatibility and stable measurement quality.
\item The Infineon TLE9012DQU forms the measurement backbone of the pack. In the laboratory build it supervises four series-connected cells with buffered RC filtering and provides cell-voltage and temperature acquisition together with passive balancing capability.
\item The active front-end includes balancing circuitry, while unused monitor inputs are terminated according to the 12-to-4 cell adaptation of the reference design so that measurement integrity is preserved on the active channels.
\item Temperature instrumentation is extended beyond the standard TLE inputs by an NTC harness that can connect additional probes at cell terminals, busbars, or ambient reference points. This provides a finer spatial thermal picture during ageing and state-estimation tests.
\item The controller domain uses an STM32H755ZITx dual-core microcontroller. This design choice is central because it creates a clean architectural separation between conventional BMS functionality and the estimator layer that is investigated in the dissertation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Firmware and Execution Concept}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The firmware concept is intentionally centred on the dual-core STM32 architecture.
\item The Cortex-M4 core is used for conventional BMS functionality such as measurement handling, supervision, and general system management, whereas the Cortex-M7 core is reserved for the computationally heavier state-estimation routines.
\item This split is important for the dissertation because it makes the estimator layer exchangeable. Different SoC and SoH methods can be integrated, tested, and replaced on the M7 side without redesigning the complete BMS firmware structure.
\item The platform was built exactly for this purpose: one stable hardware and BMS basis should support repeated experimentation with different embedded-AI methods, different estimator classes, and different implementation variants.
\item From the perspective of the dissertation, the central contribution of the firmware architecture is therefore not the low-level implementation detail itself, but the fact that it creates a reusable and customisable execution environment for embedded battery state estimation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental Relevance for Embedded AI}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The BMS-STEP platform is not only a monitoring board, but an experimental platform for comparing estimator classes under realistic embedded conditions.
\item Its main value for the dissertation lies in controllability and openness: the same measurement hardware can be reused while state-estimation methods are changed, refined, compressed, or replaced.
\item This makes it possible to compare classical model-based observers and data-driven or neural approaches on one common embedded basis instead of distributing them across unrelated hardware environments.
\item The BMS-STEP platform therefore closes the gap between algorithm development and embedded validation. It was chosen precisely because it can be modified and extended in ways that would be difficult with a purchased closed BMS.
\item At the same time, the 4s implementation remains deliberately compact. It serves as a reproducible laboratory baseline from which later transfer to larger stacks or a 16s/\SI{48}{\volt} variant can be reasoned without changing the overall control and estimator architecture.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Neural Network Architecture for Ageing Estimation in Stationary Storage Systems}
\section{Application Context and Related Work}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item State-of-health estimation is a central prerequisite for reliable stationary storage operation, especially in smart-grid and microgrid settings in which storage units must remain available over long maintenance intervals and under fluctuating renewable-power conditions.\autocite{sahriEnergyManagementSystem2021,solimanSupervisoryEnergyManagement2021}
\item Within this field, the literature usually distinguishes between model-based and data-driven SOH estimation. Model-based methods exploit explicit physical battery descriptions, whereas data-driven methods learn the relation between measured operating variables and the degradation state directly from data. The data-driven route is chosen here because it is better suited to strongly nonlinear ageing behaviour and does not require the same level of electrochemical model knowledge for practical deployment.\autocite{gouStateofHealthEstimationRemainingUsefulLife2020,ojiDataDrivenMethodsBattery2021}
\item Representative model-based alternatives include Kalman-filter-based joint SOC/SOH estimation, particle-filter-based ageing tracking, and fractional-order ageing models. Reported errors in this group range from about \SI{1}{\percent} for dual adaptive Kalman-filter approaches to around \SI{3}{\percent} for capacity-fade estimation and \SI{4}{\percent} for power-fade estimation, while fractional-order-model studies report remaining-capacity errors near \SI{3.1}{\percent}. These approaches can therefore achieve strong nominal accuracy, but they also depend on parameter identification, filter tuning, and, in some cases, computationally demanding physical formulations.\autocite{liJointEstimationState2021,biOnlineStateHealth2020,tianFractionalOrderModelBasedIncremental2019}
\item On the measurement-driven and machine-learning side, the discussion covers pressure-based abnormality detection, partial-voltage and incremental-capacity methods, hybrid deep-learning--Kalman combinations, autoregressive prediction, support-vector-machine approaches, fuzzy-entropy features, and SOC-domain preprocessing. Direct-measurement approaches based on reduced charging-voltage windows report average errors below \SI{2.5}{\percent}, and hybrid deep-learning--Kalman combinations reduce estimation errors to about \SI{2}{\percent}. At the same time, these studies reveal typical limitations such as sensitivity to selected operating windows, dependence on feature validity, susceptibility to abnormal data points, and limited transferability to varying operating conditions.\autocite{ebertDetectionCellstackInhomogeneities2017,stroeStateofHealthEstimationLithiumIon2018,stroeSOHEstimationLMO2018,shiOptimizationStateCharge2021,kimImprovedStateofhealthPrediction2022,xiongOnlineStateofHealthEstimation2021,suiRobustFuzzyEntropyBased2022,joBatteryStateofHealthEstimation2021}
\item More recent hybrid and sequence-based approaches process raw voltage, current, and temperature histories directly by neural networks or combine data-driven inference with physics-informed components. Sequence-to-sequence hybrid degradation prediction has, for example, reported mean absolute percentage errors below \SI{2.5}{\percent} for the capacity trajectory and \SI{6.5}{\percent} for the remaining usable cycles when only \SI{20}{\percent} of the data are used, with further reductions to below \SI{1.3}{\percent} and \SI{5}{\percent} when \SI{50}{\percent} of the data are available. These approaches are methodologically close to the later development path of this dissertation. The present ageing-estimation study nevertheless starts from a deliberately simpler MLP baseline in order to test how far feature engineering and lag-sequence construction can already push a comparatively lightweight model.\autocite{xuNovelHybridPhysicsBased2023,pangPhysicsinformedNeuralNetwork2023,kimEstimationLiionBattery2018,chaouiAgingPredictionState2017}
\item The intention is therefore not to claim that MLPs are universally superior to recurrent or hybrid models. The central assumption is instead that a suitable representation of voltage, temperature, and current history can make a simple feedforward network surprisingly effective while keeping the model easier to train, easier to interpret structurally, and closer to later deployment-oriented questions.\autocite{makridakisStatisticalMachineLearning2018,howStateChargeEstimation2019a,hossainlipuDatadrivenStateCharge2020}
\end{itemize}

\section{Raw Cell Dataset, Parameters and Measured Quantities}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The underlying dataset originates from cyclic ageing tests on Li-ion INR18650 NMC cells carried out at Technische Universit\"at Berlin.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a}
\item The investigated cells have a nominal capacity of \SI{2.6}{\ampere\hour}, a nominal voltage of \SI{3.7}{\volt}, a maximum charging voltage of \SI{4.2}{\volt}, a discharge cut-off voltage of \SI{2.75}{\volt}, and a maximum continuous discharge current of 5C.
\item In total, 14 fresh cells are analysed. They are grouped into seven ageing scenarios with two cells per scenario, and all cells start from an initial SOH of \SI{100}{\percent}.
\item The scenarios vary four operating parameters: the mean state of charge, the depth of discharge, the discharge C-rate, and the operating temperature. These parameters are selected because they reproduce representative operating windows of stationary storage systems rather than highly dynamic mobile-load profiles and therefore follow the application logic discussed for standard stationary-storage profiles.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a,kucevicStandardBatteryEnergy2020a}
\item Charging is performed with a constant-current constant-voltage procedure, whereas discharging is carried out with constant current. After a defined number of ageing cycles, capacity tests are inserted to determine the degradation state of the cells, which is consistent with established battery ageing and calendar-testing practice.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a,kassemCalendarAgingGraphite2012}
\item The directly measured quantities are current, voltage, temperature, and time. Because the sampling intervals depend on the respective process step, all measurements are resampled to \SI{1}{\second} before the later feature engineering is applied.
\item One scenario with \SI{20}{\percent} mean state of charge, \SI{30}{\percent} depth of discharge, 1C discharge rate, and \SI{35}{\celsius} contains premature test termination and irregular measurements for cells 3 and 4. These data are nevertheless retained in the training pool in order to test whether meaningful ageing relations can still be learned from imperfect measurements.
\end{itemize}

\section{Preprocessing Method and Feature Engineering}
\subsection{Cumulative Current}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The preprocessing pipeline is built around the argument that instantaneous current alone is not sufficient as an explanatory variable for ageing estimation.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a}
\item Cumulative current is used as the central derived feature. This quantity integrates current over time and thereby reflects both the present loading level and the throughput-related stress that accumulates over the lifetime of the cell.
\item The original argument is explicitly energy-related: the model should not only see whether a cell is currently charging or discharging, but should also receive information about how much electrochemical throughput has already occurred within the observed history window.
\item The work further argues that charging and discharging should not simply be collapsed into one absolute current magnitude. Because charging and discharging can lead to different voltage gradients and different effective ageing signatures, the representation is designed to retain directional information as far as possible.
\item In this formulation, cumulative current becomes the feature that allows a relatively simple feedforward network to still access ageing-relevant temporal context without explicitly using a recurrent structure.
\item Voltage and temperature are retained as further direct inputs, so the final feature set combines operating-state information with a compact representation of the prior load exposure.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Estimation of the State of Health}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The SOH target is obtained from periodic capacity tests.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a,baraiComparisonMethodologiesNoninvasive2019,kurzweilElektrochemischeSpeicherSuperkondensatoren2018}
\item For each cell, the measured capacity is related to the nominal capacity so that the state of health is defined as the ratio between actual and nominal capacity.
\item The first capacity test serves as the nominal reference point, whereas the following tests describe the degradation progression over time.
\item Because current, voltage, and temperature are available continuously but the capacity tests exist only at discrete points, the SOH progression is interpolated onto the timeline of the directly recorded measurements.
\item The SOH progression is represented both over equivalent full cycles and over absolute time in days. This temporal representation is especially relevant for the neural network because it makes the long-term ageing trend visible in the same time domain from which the lag sequences are extracted.
\item The resulting SOH trajectories already reveal that temperature has a pronounced impact on degradation, whereas the interactions between the remaining operating parameters are less obvious from direct visual inspection alone.
\end{itemize}

\item To better understand which measured quantities should be retained for learning, the SOH trajectories are supplemented with a correlation analysis.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a}

\item In contrast, the time derivative of the voltage does not show a comparably direct influence in the linear correlation view, although this does not exclude non-linear relevance for neural-network-based inference.
\item The correlation analysis is therefore treated only as a starting point for feature selection and not as a replacement for the later model-based evaluation. Still, it provides a concrete justification for keeping temperature and cumulative-current descriptors in the final feature set.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Formation of the Lag Sequence}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Each learning sample is constructed as a lag sequence of voltage, temperature, and cumulative current, with the SOH at the current point in time used as the corresponding label.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a}
\item This transforms the continuous measurement stream into structured sliding-window samples that can be processed by a feedforward network without passing the complete life history of an individual cell directly into the model.
\item The initial parameterisation uses a time resolution of \SI{30}{\minute} and 12 lag steps, which corresponds to a history length of \SI{6}{\hour}.
\item The time resolution has to match the actual charging and discharging behaviour. If the spacing is too coarse, relevant dynamics disappear. If it is too fine, the model sees an unnecessarily long and noisy representation that can make training less effective.
\item This design can also be linked to the sampling theorem: the spacing between data points has to remain compatible with the temporal structure of the charging and discharging cycles so that the network can still recognise the relevant periodic behaviour.\autocite{puenteleonSignaleUndSysteme2015}
\item Because the raw measurements are resampled before sequence generation, the preprocessing step also acts like a low-pass filter and reduces the effect of high-frequency noise components.
\item A further benefit of the lag-sequence approach is that the training samples are no longer tied to one specific cell trajectory. Instead of memorising individual cells, the model is trained on many local windows drawn from the full dataset.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training, Validation, and Test Splits}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The available data are separated into training, validation, and test subsets.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a}
\item During training, 20\% of the available training sequences are reserved for validation so that the fitting process can be monitored for overfitting and generalisation behaviour.
\item At the same time, several complete cells are withheld from the training process for the final performance assessment. In total, four out of fourteen cells are retained for this independent accuracy evaluation.
\item This choice is important because the method is intended to generalise to previously unseen cells and not only to previously unseen windows extracted from known cells.
\item The resulting process chain therefore mirrors the actual intended use case: build the model on one group of cells, monitor generalisation on validation data, and finally evaluate the trained estimator on cells that were not part of the optimisation loop.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data Scaling}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item All input features are scaled to the interval between 0 and 1 before training.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a}
\item This step is necessary because the numerical ranges of the features differ strongly. Voltage remains in a narrow interval between \SI{2.75}{\volt} and \SI{4.2}{\volt}, whereas cumulative current can rise to several thousand ampere-hours across the full ageing experiment.
\item Uniform scaling improves numerical stability, supports convergence during optimisation, and avoids a situation in which large-magnitude inputs dominate the network weights while smaller but still relevant signals are effectively ignored.\autocite{salimansWeightNormalizationSimple2016}
\end{itemize}

\section{Multilayer Perceptron Neural Network Model Structure}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The selected model type is a multilayer perceptron with fully connected layers.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a}
\item A convolutional interpretation is not pursued because the data do not exhibit a spatial image-like structure. Recurrent architectures such as LSTMs are also not used at this stage, because the temporal dependence is already encoded through the lag-sequence construction and because the recurrent alternatives are more resource-intensive and more difficult to tune in the chosen setting.\autocite{weytjensCashFlowPrediction2021}
\item The MLP therefore consists of an input layer, several dense hidden layers with ReLU activation, and a single linear output node for the SOH estimate.
\item The original starting point is intentionally small and uses one hidden layer with eight nodes. The final network depth and width are then determined in the hyperparameter search.
\item In the tuned configuration, the model grows into a deep dense architecture with nine hidden/output stages and a final linear regression output. The resulting structure remains conceptually simple from an architectural point of view, but it is substantially larger than the initial baseline.
\end{itemize}

\section{Configuration and Hyperparameter Tuning of the Neural Network}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Model quality is evaluated by means of mean squared error and mean absolute error.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a}
\item Mean squared error is used as the central optimisation-oriented metric during training and validation, whereas both MSE and MAE are used for the final interpretation on the held-out test cells.
\item The hyperparameter search covers the number of lag steps, the temporal resolution of the lag sequence, network depth, number of nodes, batch size, kernel regularisation, optimizer type, and activation function.
\item Hyperband is used as the main search strategy because it distributes training resources adaptively, evaluates many candidate settings quickly, and allocates more epochs only to the most promising configurations.\autocite{liHyperbandNovelBanditBased2016}
\item The study reports that ADAM outperforms RMSProp, Adadelta, and Adagrad for this task. Larger batch sizes reduce overfitting tendencies, whereas explicit kernel regularisation does not lead to a meaningful improvement.
\item The optimizer analysis is complemented by an activation-function comparison. ReLU yields the lowest loss in the tested setup, while sigmoid, tanh, and softmax perform worse.
\item The models are trained on approximately 15{,}000 sequences with varying batch-size and epoch combinations, and larger batches converge to a comparable minimum while reducing overfitting tendencies.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results and Performance Evaluation}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The evaluation shows that the two most influential sequence parameters are the number of lag steps and the temporal resolution.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a}
\item For the detailed analysis, four time resolutions are compared: \SI{1}{\minute}, \SI{10}{\minute}, \SI{30}{\minute}, and \SI{60}{\minute}. In parallel, the number of lag steps is varied between 8, 12, 16, and 20.
\item Across the exemplary cells, the most favourable time-resolution range lies between \SI{10}{\minute} and \SI{30}{\minute}. This matches the characteristic charge and discharge durations of the investigated scenarios, which typically fall in the range of approximately \SI{20}{\minute} to \SI{1}{\hour}.
\item If the time resolution is too large, relevant behaviour is averaged out and important information is lost. If it is too small, the training process becomes less effective and the longer effective histories do not translate automatically into better prediction quality.
\item The MAE matrix shows the smallest errors for 16 lag steps at a time resolution of \SI{10}{\minute}. This corresponds to a history length of \SI{160}{\minute}.
\item Extending the sequence length beyond 20 steps is reported as disadvantageous because the resulting history becomes excessively long and does not improve prediction quality further.
\end{itemize}

\section{Interpretation of the Ageing-Estimation Results}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item A meaningful SOH estimate can be obtained with a comparatively simple feedforward model when the preprocessing and feature engineering are aligned with the underlying ageing problem.\autocite{rzepkaNeuralNetworkArchitecture2023a}
\item One of the central insights is that the input representation is at least as important as the model class itself. Cumulative current, lag-sequence construction, and interpolation of the SOH reference are decisive parts of the overall method.
\item The results simultaneously show the dependence of neural SOH estimation on the available dataset. Atypical ageing trajectories, interrupted tests, and limited data diversity remain the dominant constraints on generalisation.
\item Even under these limitations, the approach reaches a level of accuracy that remains competitive with more complex alternatives. This makes it a suitable starting point for the dissertation, because it establishes a first data-driven estimator that is simple enough to connect later to questions of robustness, compression, and embedded deployment.
\item The method is not restricted to one narrowly defined technology, because it relies only on directly measured quantities such as voltage, temperature, and current-derived throughput features. In principle, the concept can therefore be transferred to other cells and chemistries if sufficiently rich data are available.
\item At the same time, the results point directly toward the next development steps: broader datasets and systematic comparisons with CNN- and LSTM-based alternatives are necessary to test whether the proposed lag-sequence concept remains advantageous across different model classes.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Robustness Benchmarking of Embedded Battery State Estimation}
\section{Application Context and Objective}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item This chapter investigates deployment-facing battery-state estimation under realistic Battery Management System conditions rather than under clean laboratory input signals alone.
\item The central objective is a measurement-only robustness benchmark in which estimator performance is isolated from broader system-level effects such as hardware-resource optimization or implementation-effort comparison.
\item Practical deployment quality is not defined by clean-condition accuracy alone, because real BMS operation is exposed to sensor bias, additive noise, missing samples, irregular timing, restart-related initialization mismatch, and transient signal spikes caused by the measurement chain or by communication faults.
\item In this benchmark, estimator quality is therefore defined as the joint behavior under nominal baseline conditions, under initialization mismatch, and under disturbed measurements or signal-integrity faults.
\item The chapter compares four embedded-ready estimator classes on the same LFP full-cell trajectory and under the same disturbance protocol: a direct-measurement model (DM), a hybrid direct-measurement model with learned SOH support (HDM), a hybrid equivalent-circuit model with voltage feedback (HECM), and a data-driven recurrent model with GRU-based SOC estimation and LSTM-based online SOH context (DD).
\item Under clean conditions, HDM provides the lowest baseline error with an MAE of 0.0024, followed by HECM with 0.0090, DD with 0.0100, and DM with 0.0311. Under disturbed measurements, however, the ranking changes substantially, which is the core reason for the benchmark.
\item The benchmark therefore shifts the evaluation logic away from single clean-condition error values and toward a more realistic view in which sensing imperfections, missing updates, and recovery after corrupted inputs become part of the core assessment.
\end{itemize}

\section{State of the Art and Benchmark Gap}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The relevant literature usually distinguishes between direct-measurement or integration-based estimators, model-based estimators built around equivalent-circuit or electrochemical structure, hybrid approaches, and purely data-driven sequence models.
\item Direct-measurement approaches are attractive because they are simple, computationally inexpensive, and easy to deploy, but they are structurally exposed to current bias, missing data, and initialization errors because they do not contain an internal correction mechanism.
\item Equivalent-circuit and observer-based estimators offer physical interpretability and voltage-based correction capability, but their practical performance depends strongly on parameter quality, observability, and measurement quality.
\item Hybrid approaches combine physical state propagation with learned adaptation of capacity, parameters, or auxiliary states and are therefore attractive for joint SOC/SOH estimation, but they do not automatically remove disturbance sensitivity.
\item Data-driven recurrent estimators can exploit nonlinear relations between voltage, current, temperature, and derived online variables, and recent work has also shown their embedded feasibility through pruning, quantization, and TinyML-style deployment.
\item The main benchmark gap is that much of the literature still emphasizes clean-data accuracy, average error metrics, or computational efficiency, while comparatively few studies compare fundamentally different estimator classes under the same disturbance protocol and under matched embedded-ready execution conditions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Estimator Classes Under Test}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The benchmark compares four embedded-ready estimator classes under matched online execution conditions.
\item DM represents the canonical Coulomb-counting baseline with fixed nominal capacity and explicit full-charge reset logic. It is the low-complexity reference for integration-driven SOC estimation.
\item HDM preserves the same Coulomb-counting core but replaces the fixed capacity assumption with an online effective capacity obtained from a shared SOH estimator. In structural terms, it differs from DM only in the denominator of the SOC mapping and therefore represents a lightweight hybrid estimator.
\item HECM represents the physics-based observer class. It is implemented as a second-order RC model with the state vector $\mathbf{x}_k = [\widehat{\mathrm{SOC}}_k, U_{1,k}, U_{2,k}]^\top$, current-driven state propagation, voltage-residual correction, and SOH-dependent parameter scheduling.
\item DD represents the compact recurrent neural estimator class. It uses a stateful GRU-based SOC model with the feature vector $\{U, I, T, \mathrm{SOH}, Q_c, dU/dt, dI/dt, \Delta t\}$, a single-layer GRU with hidden size 96, an MLP head with one hidden layer of size 96, and a sigmoid output layer.
\item All SOH-aware variants share the same LSTM-based online SOH estimator, so that the comparison focuses on how the estimator classes consume the shared SOH information rather than on unrelated SOH-model differences.
\item The shared SOH estimator operates on hourly aggregated online features derived from $\{U, I, T, \mathrm{EFC}, Q_c\}$ using mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum, which yields a 20-dimensional hourly feature vector. Architecturally, it consists of a feature embedding block of size 128, a two-layer unidirectional LSTM with hidden size 160, and a prediction head with hidden size 160.
\item During benchmark execution, the SOH estimate is updated at hourly cadence and then held piecewise constant between update points. In HDM it rescales the effective capacity, in HECM it affects the capacity and lookup-based parameterization, and in DD it is provided as an explicit causal input feature.
\item The direct-measurement variants use the same online cumulative charge state $Q_c$, rebuilt causally from the measured current and the sampling interval. HDM replaces the fixed nominal capacity with the effective capacity relation $C_{\mathrm{eff}} = C_{\mathrm{nom}} \cdot \widehat{\mathrm{SOH}}$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Embedded-Oriented Model Preparation}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Although the central focus of the chapter is estimator performance and not hardware optimization, all implementations are selected and prepared under a common embedded feasibility requirement corresponding to an STM32H755ZI-class platform with \SI{1}{MB} flash and \SI{1}{MB} RAM.
\item DM and HECM are already compact by construction because they rely on scalar charge-state updates and lookup-based observer logic. For the neural components, deployment-prepared variants are used.
\item These compact neural variants correspond to structured width reduction, short post-pruning finetuning, and INT8 weight quantization, yielding hidden size 67 for the SOC-GRU and hidden size 112 for the SOH-LSTM in the deployment-prepared versions.
\item The component-level footprint estimates in the paper are 4.2 KiB flash and 16 B for the DM core, 27.9 KiB flash and 268 B for the SOC-GRU core, 408.3 KiB flash and 1792 B for the SOH-LSTM core, and 442.5 KiB flash and 52 B for the HECM core.
\item Aggregated to estimator level, the reported persistent footprints are 4.2 KiB flash and 16 B RAM for DM, 412.5 KiB and 1808 B for HDM, 850.8 KiB and 1844 B for HECM, and 436.2 KiB and 2060 B for DD.
\item These values matter because the benchmark does not compare unconstrained large neural models against inherently compact classical estimators. All four variants remain within the same reference deployment budget.
\end{itemize}

\section{Measurement-Only Benchmark Principle}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The benchmark follows a strict measurement-only principle.
\item Only quantities that a real BMS measures directly or reconstructs online from measured data are manipulated, namely current, voltage, temperature, sample availability, and sampling time.
\item Artificial hidden-state corruption or free synthetic parameter mismatch are deliberately excluded, because the benchmark is intended to remain physically interpretable and reproducible from the perspective of practical BMS sensing.
\item Under this design, all estimators process the same disturbed input stream online and rebuild their auxiliary features causally from the corrupted measurements, including cumulative charge, derivative channels, and SOH-related context where applicable.
\item The benchmark should therefore be read as a controlled cross-class estimator study and not as a full production-BMS validation campaign. Its role is to isolate estimator-side failure modes under matched data, matched disturbance definitions, matched online execution rules, and matched embedded-feasibility constraints.
\item This ensures that the observed differences can be attributed to estimator structure and disturbance propagation instead of to mismatched preprocessing or access to unavailable information.
\end{itemize}

\section{Disturbance Taxonomy and Scenario Design}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The disturbance space is grouped into input disturbances, initialization errors, and signal-integrity faults.
\item The implemented scenarios are ADC quantization, current bias, additive current noise, voltage noise, temperature noise, initial SOC mismatch, missing samples, irregular sampling, burst dropout, and voltage spikes.
\item ADC quantization is implemented with step sizes of \SI{0.01}{A} for current, \SI{0.005}{V} for voltage, and \SI{0.5}{\celsius} for temperature.
\item Current bias is implemented as a constant offset of \SI{0.05}{A} in the compact matrix and additionally as a dedicated sweep of 0.5\%, 1.5\%, and 3.0\% of $|I|_{\max}$.
\item Current noise is modelled as Gaussian noise with $\sigma_I=\SI{0.10}{A}$ in the compact matrix and is extended to a local-detail sweep up to $\sigma_I=\SI{0.20}{A}$.
\item Voltage noise is implemented as additive Gaussian noise with $\sigma_U=\SI{0.01}{V}$, and temperature noise as additive Gaussian noise with $\sigma_T=\SI{1.0}{\celsius}$.
\item The initialization scenario applies an additive initial mismatch of $-0.10$ SOC. For DD this is realized as a perturbation of the online cumulative-charge feature $Q_c$ rather than as an explicit latent-state reset.
\item Missing samples are implemented by freezing every 50th sample on the nominal \SI{1}{s} grid. Irregular sampling uses uniform timing jitter of $\pm\SI{0.1}{s}$ around the nominal \SI{1}{s} grid.
\item Burst dropout is implemented as one central frozen gap of \SI{3600}{s}, and the voltage-spike scenario injects one single-sample spike of $\pm\SI{0.20}{V}$ every 1000 samples, corresponding to every \SI{1000}{s} on the nominal time base.
\item These scenarios are selected because they represent sensor limitations, adverse measurement corruption, and deliberate signal-integrity stress cases that remain meaningful for embedded BMS deployment.
\item Current bias primarily exposes long-term integration drift and sensor miscalibration. Voltage noise primarily stresses the correction pathway of physics-based and hybrid estimators. Missing samples, burst dropout, and irregular sampling expose the behavior under frozen updates, delayed information, and timing uncertainty.
\item The disturbance taxonomy is therefore not only a list of corruptions. It is a structured way of exposing different failure mechanisms such as drift, loss of correction, delayed resynchronization, transient instability, or persistent post-disturbance error.
\end{itemize}

\section{Metrics and Evaluation Logic}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The benchmark evaluates three performance dimensions: nominal accuracy, convergence behavior after state inconsistency, and robustness under sustained or transient measurement corruption.
\item Global disturbed-operation performance is quantified through MAE, RMSE, bias, maximum error, and the $95$th percentile absolute error. These metrics summarize average accuracy, systematic deviation, and tail behavior across the complete trajectory.
\item Local disturbance performance is additionally analysed through scenario-specific quantities such as output variance, excess rolling MAE, recovery time, jump behavior, and aligned post-disturbance error, because several scenarios do not primarily manifest as a collapse of full-run MAE.
\item Whenever a disturbance mask exists, the analysis separates pre-disturbance, disturbed, and post-disturbance behavior in order to quantify disturbance-window error growth, recovery time, and residual post-disturbance error.
\item In the current benchmark phase, ADC quantization, current bias, voltage noise, temperature noise, missing samples, and irregular sampling are interpreted mainly through global metrics, whereas burst dropout, voltage spikes, current-noise detail, and initialization mismatch additionally require local evidence.
\item Recovery after initialization errors is interpreted through two baseline-relative operating bands rather than through one universal fixed threshold. The strict band is defined as $\max(\mathrm{P95}_{\mathrm{base}}, 1.5 \cdot \mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{base}})$.
\item The fair band is defined as $\max(2 \cdot \mathrm{P95}_{\mathrm{base}}, 3 \cdot \mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{base}}, 0.02)$ and acts as a more deployment-tolerant criterion for estimators that re-enter a practically acceptable operating range without immediately returning to their strict near-baseline regime.
\item Both recovery bands require sustained re-entry over a fixed horizon so that the reported recovery times reflect stable resynchronization rather than an accidental short crossing of the threshold.
\item This multi-scale metric design evaluates performance as a structured combination of nominal accuracy, convergence behavior, global degradation, local disturbance response, and long-term drift.
\end{itemize}

\section{Dataset Basis and Online Execution Setup}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The benchmark is based on an LFP 18650 laboratory dataset recorded in the context of the MG-FARM project, which provides long measured full-cell trajectories together with sufficient structure for reproducible disturbance injection and reference-target generation.\autocite{lfpDoeCycleAgingDataset2025}
\item In the current benchmark phase, one representative full-cell trajectory is used in order to isolate estimator behavior under controlled disturbance scenarios before extending the study to additional cells and cross-cell validation.
\item The available raw channels are current, voltage, temperature, and time. Offline reference targets for SOC and SOH are derived from charge integration, capacity-test information, and interpolated degradation progression.
\item In the preprocessing of the paper, the signed transferred charge is computed as $Q_k = I_k \Delta t_k / 3600$, the absolute transferred charge as $|Q_k|$, and the reference capacity as $C_{\mathrm{ref}} = \sum_k |Q_k|$ during dedicated capacity-test intervals.
\item The offline SOH target is then defined as $\mathrm{SOH}_{\mathrm{ref}} = C_{\mathrm{ref}} / C_{\mathrm{ref},0}$, where $C_{\mathrm{ref},0}$ denotes the first measured reference capacity of the trajectory. The reference SOC target corresponds to the offline Coulomb-counting target $\mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{ref}} = 1 + Q_c / C_{\mathrm{ref},0}$.
\item All compared estimators are executed under online conditions. Disturbed inputs are processed sample by sample, and all required auxiliary quantities are rebuilt directly from the disturbed signals rather than copied from precomputed dataset columns.
\item In freeze and dropout scenarios, the disturbance propagation is handled consistently by freezing or distorting the derived online features together with the corresponding measured inputs, which is necessary for a physically meaningful comparison between model classes.
\item This is one of the main reasons why the benchmark remains close to later embedded deployment: the estimators are not evaluated as offline regressors, but as causal state estimators under measurement-side constraints.
\end{itemize}

\section{Baseline Performance}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Under nominal baseline conditions, the hybrid direct-measurement model achieves the lowest error on the selected benchmark trajectory.
\item The reported baseline metrics are: DM with MAE 0.0311, RMSE 0.0397, P95 error 0.0800, and bias 0.0311; HDM with MAE 0.0024, RMSE 0.0061, P95 error 0.0094, and bias 0.0022; HECM with MAE 0.0090, RMSE 0.0127, P95 error 0.0241, and bias -0.0029; DD with MAE 0.0100, RMSE 0.0143, P95 error 0.0336, and bias -0.0062.
\item This ranking is important because it shows immediately that the clean-condition leader is not the same as the overall robustness leader in the disturbed scenarios that follow.
\item The baseline results therefore establish the necessary reference point for interpreting error growth, recovery thresholds, and scenario-specific ranking changes, but they do not already determine the final deployment-oriented ranking.
\end{itemize}

\section{Current-Bias and Noise Sensitivity}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Current bias is one of the most discriminative scenarios in the entire benchmark.
\item Across the matched current-bias sweep of 0.5\%, 1.5\%, and 3.0\% of $|I|_{\max}$, HECM is the strongest estimator, DD is intermediate, and the two direct-measurement variants degrade strongly because persistent bias accumulates directly through the integration path.
\item In the reported current-bias scenario, HECM reaches MAE 0.0932, compared with 0.2859 for DD, 0.3068 for HDM, and 0.3143 for DM.
\item The corresponding P95 errors are 0.2954 for HECM, 0.6367 for DD, 0.6668 for HDM, and 0.6670 for DM, which shows that the difference is not only an average-effect artifact.
\item This result makes the structural distinction between integration-driven and correction-capable estimators directly visible: explicit voltage-based correction suppresses the long-term drift that dominates the direct-measurement variants.
\item Current noise is weaker than current bias in terms of global MAE, but it remains informative through local behavior and output variability.
\item Under the high-noise case with $\sigma_I=\SI{0.10}{A}$, the MAE changes are -0.0001 for DM, +0.0019 for HDM, +0.0075 for HECM, and +0.0009 for DD. HECM therefore shows the largest global penalty in this scenario.
\item The local noise story differs from the global metric. In the appendix metrics, the mean excess rolling MAE is -0.0001 for DM, 0.0019 for HDM, 0.0073 for HECM, and 0.0009 for DD. This means that HECM is most sensitive in the error domain, whereas DD shows strong disturbance propagation through several derived input channels.
\item Voltage noise with $\sigma_U=\SI{0.01}{V}$ is one of the strongest discriminators of the entire benchmark. HECM remains essentially unchanged with $\Delta\mathrm{MAE}\approx 0$, whereas DM increases by 0.0213, HDM by 0.0727, and DD by 0.0597.
\item Temperature noise with $\sigma_T=\SI{1.0}{\celsius}$ is almost neutral for DM and HECM, but increases MAE by 0.0043 for HDM and by 0.0081 for DD.
\item Read together, these results show that disturbance relevance depends strongly on the coupling between the corrupted measurement channel and the internal correction logic of the estimator.
\end{itemize}

\section{Initialization Mismatch and Recovery}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Initialization mismatch must be interpreted locally, because global MAE hides the actual resynchronization behavior after a wrong initial state.
\item The benchmark injects an initial SOC mismatch of $-0.10$. For DD, the disturbance is implemented as a perturbation of the online cumulative-charge feature rather than as an artificial latent-state reset, which keeps the test aligned with deployment-time observables.

\item Under the fair criterion, DM returns after 0.2500 h, HECM after 1.1564 h, and DD after 1.0014 h, while HDM again does not re-enter in the inspected window.

\section{Interpretation of the Robustness Results}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The direct-measurement model behaves as the expected drift-prone reference. Persistent current disturbances accumulate directly into the SOC estimate, and missing information cannot be corrected through an additional feedback path.
\item HDM improves nominal calibration by adapting the effective capacity through online SOH support, but it remains structurally exposed to the same current-integration failure mode and additionally inherits the dynamics of the learned SOH assistance.
\item HECM benefits from interpretable parameterization, explicit voltage-residual correction, and causal observer structure. This explains why it is the overall robustness leader in the most important current-bias and voltage-noise scenarios.
\item DD behaves as a compact context-rich estimator with channel-dependent strengths and weaknesses. It is especially strong under missing samples and initialization mismatch, but more exposed to voltage spikes and to noise propagation through derivative-related features.
\item A central insight of the chapter is therefore that low clean-condition MAE does not imply strong disturbed-operation performance. The trade-off is not only between accuracy and computational complexity, but also between calibration strength, correction capability, recovery speed, and disturbance propagation.
\item The benchmark also shows that estimator comparison should not collapse everything into one leaderboard. A deployment-oriented comparison must separate nominal accuracy, disturbance robustness, recovery after inconsistency, and implementation feasibility.
\end{itemize}

\section{Decision-Oriented Deployment Interpretation}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item If lowest nominal SOC error under clean sensing is the main design priority, HDM is the recommended class because it achieves the lowest baseline MAE of 0.0024. The corresponding trade-off is strong sensitivity to current bias and the lack of recovery to the baseline bands in the inspected initialization-mismatch window.
\item If the primary priority is best overall robustness under uncertain measurement quality, HECM is the strongest choice because it is most resilient under current bias, remains nearly unchanged under voltage noise, and still preserves competitive baseline accuracy. The trade-off is the larger flash footprint and slower strict-band recovery than DD.
\item If fastest resynchronization after restart or wrong initial state dominates the design, DD is the strongest option because it shows the fastest strict-band recovery in the local initialization analysis. The trade-off is greater sensitivity to voltage spikes and weaker performance than HECM under current bias.
\item If resilience to missing samples and signal freezing dominates the design, DD again becomes attractive because it shows the smallest MAE increase under missing samples and strong post-disturbance stability. The trade-off is that clean-condition accuracy remains below HDM and voltage-driven disturbances require a careful feature-pipeline design.
\item If the main objective is maximum implementation simplicity and the largest resource margin, DM remains the simplest class by far, but it also shows the weakest robustness under biased current measurements and missing-sample stress.
\item The practical message of the chapter is therefore not that one estimator wins universally. The message is that estimator selection must be tied to the disturbance profile, recovery requirements, and implementation constraints of the target BMS.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and Future Extensions}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The current benchmark phase is based on one representative dataset trajectory and should later be extended to additional cells, broader ageing states, and cross-cell validation before stronger generalization claims are made.
\item The spike study currently uses isolated spike events. Clustered disturbances, longer spike sequences, and hardware-level disturbance injection may reveal additional model differences.
\item The initialization test for DD remains an online-state proxy based on $Q_c$ perturbation rather than an explicit latent-state reset. This is methodologically justified, but it should still be interpreted as a class-specific approximation.
\item Future extensions should include the remaining extended scenarios, additional cells, pack-level settings, and eventually hardware-in-the-loop or true embedded runtime benchmarks under the same performance-oriented evaluation philosophy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Embedded Deployment Objective}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item This chapter examines how SOC and SOH estimators can be transferred from floating-point research models to practical microcontroller execution.
\item The central contribution lies in the complete deployment path from trained recurrent models to executable MCU firmware using structured pruning, INT8 weight quantization, manual export, and on-device benchmark measurements on an STM32H753ZI.
\item In contrast to purely algorithmic model comparisons, the focus here is on the interaction between predictive utility, resource demand, and implementation effort on the target hardware.
\item The paper therefore treats battery AI explicitly as an embedded systems problem: an estimator is only useful if it remains accurate under long-horizon streaming replay and still fits into the flash, SRAM, runtime, and energy budget of the controller.
\item The chapter follows the paper closely and transfers the original logic almost one-to-one: dataset basis, feature engineering, stateful LSTM architecture, pruning, quantization, firmware export, STM32 benchmarking, and the resulting multi-objective trade-offs.

\section{Dataset Basis and Experimental Scope}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item All experiments rely on the published LFP DoE Cycle Ageing Dataset, which was created at Technische Universit\"at Berlin on commercially available JGCFR18650-1800mAh-3.2V LFP cells.\autocite{lfpDoeCycleAgingDataset2025}
\item The ageing campaign follows a design-of-experiments scheme in a climatic chamber at \SI{25}{\celsius}. The three varied factors are the charge rate $C_{\mathrm{chg}}\in[0.5\,\mathrm{C},0.9\,\mathrm{C}]$, the discharge rate $C_{\mathrm{dis}}\in[1.0\,\mathrm{C},2.5\,\mathrm{C}]$, and the depth of discharge $\mathrm{DoD}\in[45\%,65\%]$.
\item A mixed full/fractional factorial plan assigns 15 test cells to eight operating points, with duplicate conditions for reproducibility. Between ageing loops, dedicated check-ups with capacity tests, open-circuit-voltage sweeps, and hybrid pulse-power characterisation are inserted.
\item In addition to controlled cycling, stationary-storage load profiles derived from residential PV home storage are injected so that the data contain both laboratory diagnostics and application-oriented operating conditions.
\item The raw measurements are available at \SI{1}{\hertz} and contain voltage, current, terminal temperature, and auxiliary channels. This combination is important because it exposes the estimators to both fast SOC transients and slow SOH evolution across long ageing horizons.
\end{itemize}

\section{Feature Engineering and Reference Targets}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The SOC estimator uses the six-dimensional feature vector $\bigl[U(t), I(t), T(t), Q_c(t), \mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}t, \mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}t\bigr]$, where the temporal derivatives are computed by centred finite differences.
\item Each SOC feature is robustly normalised with median and interquartile range so that the exact same scaling statistics can be embedded into the firmware.
\item The SOH estimator uses a different feature stack that reflects the slower degradation dynamics: $\bigl[t, U(t), I(t), T(t), \mathrm{EFC}(t), Q_c(t)\bigr]$, where $\mathrm{EFC}(t)$ denotes equivalent full cycles.
\item The SOC reference is derived from Coulomb counting with drift compensation. After each constant-voltage termination the integral is reset to $\mathrm{SOC}=1$, which reduces long-term bias caused by capacity fade.
\item The SOH reference is defined from periodic capacity check-ups as $C_k/C_{\mathrm{nom}}$, that is, the measured discharge capacity relative to nominal capacity.
\item Both reference traces are explicitly offline constructs. They rely on information that is not available in deployment, such as long rest periods for OCV stabilisation or periodic full-capacity measurements. On the STM32 the estimators operate only on streamed measurements and internal states.
\item The paper then uses a strictly stateful streaming regime: hidden and cell states are propagated sample by sample along the full sequence, without sliding windows. Host-side replay and STM32 replay therefore follow identical inference conditions.
\end{itemize}

\section{LSTM Architecture and Training Setup}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Both SOC and SOH estimators employ the same recurrent equation set, but with task-specific size. The SOC model uses an LSTM with hidden size $H=64$ and an MLP output head of width 64, whereas the SOH model uses hidden size 128 and an MLP width of 128.
\item This restriction to a single-layer LSTM\,+\,MLP architecture is deliberate. The paper argues that the main research focus is not broad architecture search, but a transparent and reproducible embedded tool flow for stateful estimators.
\item Training is carried out on NVIDIA A100-class GPU hardware in mixed precision (FP16). Both tasks use the AdamW optimiser, cosine-annealing warm restarts, gradient clipping, and mini-batch training.
\item For SOC, the learning rate is $1.5\times10^{-4}$, the batch size is 512, and the sequence chunk length is 2024. For SOH, the learning rate is $2.0\times10^{-4}$, the batch size is 128, and the chunk length is 2048.
\item Although deployment is fully stateful and step-wise, training uses truncated backpropagation through time over these chunks. Within each chunk, the hidden and cell states are propagated exactly as in deployment so that the stateful update mechanism is already learned during optimisation.
\item The abstracted performance envelope reported by the paper is already informative: over long-horizon streaming replay, the three SOC variants achieve MAE values between \SI{2.3}{\percent} and \SI{2.8}{\percent} of full scale, while the SOH variants reach MAE values between \SI{0.9}{\percent} and \SI{1.5}{\percent}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Structured Pruning for MCU Deployment}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Pruning is applied as structured magnitude pruning at the level of complete LSTM hidden units.
\item The saliency score aggregates the L2 norms of the incoming weights for each hidden unit across all four LSTM gates. Hidden units with the smallest scores are removed first.
\item In the final deployment, the SOC estimator is reduced from 64 to 45 hidden units and the SOH estimator from 128 to 90 hidden units. In both cases this corresponds to roughly 30\% channel-wise sparsity.
\item Rows and columns are removed jointly from the recurrent gate matrices and the downstream MLP weights so that the pruned network remains a valid stateful recurrent mapping.
\item After each pruning step the model is fine-tuned again. This is important because the paper does not treat pruning as a purely static compression step, but as a repeated prune-and-recover cycle.
\item The engineering purpose of pruning is direct: reduce recurrent state size, shrink flash and RAM demand, and shorten on-device inference time without materially compromising streaming accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\section{INT8 Quantization and Manual STM32 Export}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Quantization complements pruning by reducing the numerical precision of the remaining weights while leaving the topology unchanged.
\item The paper uses post-training per-row symmetric INT8 quantization for the input-to-gate and hidden-to-gate matrices of the LSTM. Each row receives its own floating-point scale factor, while biases and activations remain in FP32.
\item This design choice is pragmatic: it preserves numerical stability and keeps the firmware implementation close to the PyTorch reference while still reducing recurrent weight storage by roughly a factor of four relative to FP32 storage.
\item The embedded gate computation reconstructs the effective weights from INT8 codes and FP32 scales during inference. A built-in streaming comparison validates the conversion and typically yields MAE differences below $10^{-4}$ between exported and reference implementations.
\item The exported model parameters are written as C headers and integrated into manually implemented stateful LSTM\,+\,MLP kernels. This manual route is chosen because STM32Cube.AI does not natively support the continuous state handling required for these estimators.
\item The result is a fully transparent firmware path: every scaling constant, every recurrent state update, and every arithmetic step can be audited and reproduced outside the proprietary toolchain.
\end{itemize}

\section{Benchmark Methodology and KPI Logic}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The benchmark is executed on an STM32 Nucleo-144 board with an STM32H753ZI microcontroller, providing \SI{2}{\mega\byte} flash and \SI{1}{\mega\byte} SRAM.
\item For each deployment, identical feature streams from the LFP dataset are replayed both on the host and on the STM32 so that accuracy, latency, and resource measurements refer to the same stateful input sequence.
\item The host-side replay operates directly on the reference PyTorch implementation and produces MAE, RMSE, $R^2$, parity views, and long-horizon trajectory comparisons.
\item The embedded benchmark injects the same feature vectors via UART, measures end-to-end host latency, measures pure on-device inference time through the DWT cycle counter, and derives energy per inference from the average inference time and an empirically calibrated board power of \SI{0.5}{\watt}.
\item RAM is computed as the sum of statically allocated `.data` and `.bss` sections plus the maximum observed stack usage. Flash is taken from the linker output of the STM32CubeIDE projects.
\item The paper therefore distinguishes carefully between communication-dominated system latency and pure on-device inference time. This distinction matters because UART framing can dominate the end-to-end measurement although the actual neural computation remains well within the real-time budget.
\end{itemize}

\section{Streaming Prediction Accuracy}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The results section of the paper starts with full-sequence streaming replay over the complete ageing trajectory.
\item For SOC, the tracking remains tight across the entire dynamic range. The residuals stay centred around zero, and the quantized model overlaps the full-precision baseline so closely that the two traces are often visually almost indistinguishable.
\item The pruned SOC model also remains highly accurate, but shows slightly stronger smoothing in highly dynamic regions. The paper interprets this as the expected effect of the reduced hidden-state dimensionality.
\item For SOH, the task is structurally harder because the estimator has to infer slow degradation from sparse check-up events and continuous monitoring data. The paper therefore removes the discrete capacity tests from the estimator input and retains only the remaining check-up blocks and operating segments.
\item A causal smoothing stage is applied to the raw SOH predictions. Specifically, the paper uses an exponential moving average with smoothing factor $\alpha=10^{-6}$ together with a downward-rate limiter of $2\times10^{-8}$ per step to suppress unphysical jumps while maintaining the monotonic degradation trend.
\item After this filtering, the pruned and quantized SOH models still track the dense baseline with similar fidelity and preserve the long-term trend despite visible differences in small-scale variability.
\end{itemize}

\section{Error Distribution and Local Transient Behaviour}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The SOC error distribution is narrow and symmetric, which indicates the absence of systematic bias.
\item The paper reports that the pruned SOC model achieves the smallest median MAE and the narrowest interquartile range, which means that pruning slightly improves the SOC task while simultaneously reducing runtime and memory demand.
\item For SOH, the error distribution is broader, which is consistent with the greater intrinsic difficulty of estimating capacity fade from operational data. Even so, the bulk of the SOH errors remains within a practically usable tolerance band of approximately $\pm 2\%$.
\item To avoid hiding local failures behind global metrics, the paper additionally zooms into a pulse segment between 30k and 40k seconds and into a representative check-up sequence between 657k and 897k seconds.
\item In the pulse region, the quantized model follows the reference transients with high fidelity and captures voltage-recovery phases accurately, whereas the pruned model smooths these sharp features slightly.
\item In the check-up phase, all models converge well and remain stable over long integration horizons, which confirms that the stateful streaming formulation does not accumulate visible drift over extended operation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Resource Efficiency, Latency, and Trade-Off Summary}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The KPI tables already show that pruning and quantization affect SOC and SOH differently.
\item For SOC, pruning reduces flash from \SI{105.32}{\kilo\byte} to \SI{62.27}{\kilo\byte}, reduces inference time from \SI{1.40}{\milli\second} to \SI{0.80}{\milli\second}, and reduces energy per inference from \SI{0.70}{\milli\joule} to \SI{0.40}{\milli\joule}. This corresponds to gains of roughly 41--43\%.
\item Quantization reduces the SOC flash footprint further to \SI{52.48}{\kilo\byte}, but increases pure inference time to \SI{6.99}{\milli\second} and energy per inference to \SI{3.49}{\milli\joule} because the software kernel has to reconstruct FP32 values from INT8 codes and scales.
\item For SOH, pruning reduces flash from \SI{335.00}{\kilo\byte} to \SI{182.41}{\kilo\byte}, reduces inference time from \SI{22.73}{\milli\second} to \SI{12.72}{\milli\second}, and reduces energy per inference from \SI{11.37}{\milli\joule} to \SI{6.36}{\milli\joule}. Quantization reduces flash further to \SI{138.00}{\kilo\byte}, but raises inference time and energy to \SI{29.21}{\milli\second} and \SI{14.60}{\milli\joule}.
\item The model-size and latency plots condense the same pattern visually: quantization is the strongest lever for non-volatile memory, while pruning is the strongest lever for energy and inference time on the current hand-written STM32 implementation.
\item The paper then introduces a simple composite utility score $U$ that aggregates MAE, flash, RAM, and energy into a single normalised comparison value. By construction, the dense baseline has $U=1$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implications for MCU-Based BMS}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The central engineering outcome of the chapter is that all investigated deployments satisfy the timing constraints of typical stationary-storage BMS applications with comfortable margin.
\item Even when communication overhead is included, end-to-end SOC latencies remain below roughly \SI{20}{\milli\second} and SOH latencies below roughly \SI{50}{\milli\second}. The pure compute time on the MCU stays below \SI{1.5}{\milli\second} for dense and pruned SOC and within roughly \SIrange{12}{30}{\milli\second} for the SOH variants.
\item The chapter also makes clear that there is no universally best compression strategy. On the current STM32 implementation, pruning provides the most balanced operating point, whereas quantization is attractive mainly when flash capacity is the dominating constraint.
\item This conclusion is reflected directly in the utility score: for SOC, the pruned variant reaches $U=0.71$ and is therefore the most favourable trade-off, whereas the quantized SOC deployment reaches $U=1.83$ because the INT8 kernel increases energy strongly. For SOH, both compressed variants remain close to the baseline with $U\approx0.9$--$1.0$.
\item From the perspective of the dissertation, this chapter provides the concrete bridge between data-driven estimator design and the actual embedded controller. It shows that SOC and SOH estimators can be co-located on one STM32H7-class platform, but that architecture, compression, and firmware implementation must be selected jointly rather than sequentially.
\end{itemize}
\chapter{Architecture-Dependent Effects of Pruning and Quantization}
\section{Problem Statement}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The embedded deployment study already demonstrates that pruning and quantization can improve the feasibility of MCU-based inference.
\item The next scientific step is to determine how strongly these gains depend on the underlying estimator architecture.
\item This question is important because compression methods that work well for one model family may degrade another model family disproportionately in terms of accuracy, numerical stability, or execution behaviour.
\item A cross-architecture analysis therefore extends the embedded perspective from a single deployment pipeline toward a broader model-compression design framework.
\end{itemize}

\section{Comparison Framework}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The comparative study varies both estimator architecture and compression strategy under matched embedded evaluation conditions.
\item Relevant architecture classes include feed-forward baselines, recurrent models such as GRU or LSTM, and hybrid models with explicit state-related or physics-aware structure.
\item Relevant compression variables include structured and unstructured pruning, INT8 quantization, mixed-precision variants, and architecture-specific compression choices.
\item The evaluation combines task-specific predictive metrics with Flash, RAM, latency, and numerical-stability indicators so that compression quality is assessed as a joint property of model and hardware.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{General Discussion and Cross-Paper Synthesis}
\section{Synthesis of the Main Findings}
The cumulative findings of the dissertation show that battery-related artificial intelligence cannot be evaluated adequately within a single disciplinary dimension. Model design, disturbance robustness, embedded feasibility, and hardware integration interact directly and must therefore be interpreted together.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The ageing-estimation chapter shows that relevant battery information can already be captured with a comparatively simple network if the temporal representation and the derived features reflect the underlying electrochemical ageing process.
\item The robustness benchmark shows that this representation-oriented view is insufficient on its own, because estimator quality changes once measurement disturbances, timing irregularities, and initialization errors are introduced systematically.
\item The embedded-AI chapter shows that the ranking of estimator candidates changes once microcontroller memory, latency, and implementation effort become explicit evaluation criteria in addition to predictive quality.
\item The hardware chapter demonstrates that these algorithmic questions are not detached from the physical system. Sensor access, firmware partitioning, estimator exchangeability, and the overall validation chain determine which AI methods can actually be investigated and transferred into BMS-oriented execution environments.
\item Taken together, the completed study chapters show that the cumulative scientific gain of the dissertation lies in connecting these layers into one coherent engineering view instead of optimizing only one isolated metric.
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodological Strengths and Limitations Across the Studies}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item A key strength of the dissertation is that it spans several layers of evidence: controlled ageing data, structured disturbance benchmarks, measured embedded deployment metrics, and the BMS-STEP hardware platform for practical validation.
\item Another strength is the common engineering perspective across otherwise different studies. Ageing estimation, robustness benchmarking, and microcontroller deployment are treated as connected parts of one BMS-related research problem.
\item At the same time, the studies rely on different datasets, task definitions, and evaluation settings. Their numerical results are therefore not interchangeable in a strict one-to-one sense, but must be interpreted as complementary evidence for the same overarching design problem.
\item The data-driven chapters remain dependent on the breadth and quality of the available datasets. Atypical degradation trajectories, incomplete measurements, and limited coverage of rare operating conditions continue to restrict the generality of the reported results.
\item The embedded and hardware-related chapters are likewise shaped by the selected microcontroller targets, firmware environment, and laboratory setup. The transferability of the exact resource figures to other platforms must therefore be interpreted with appropriate engineering caution.
\end{itemize}

\section{Positioning Within the State of Research}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item A large part of the existing literature concentrates on one dimension at a time, for example on estimation accuracy, on a particular neural architecture, or on a single deployment toolchain.
\item The present dissertation contributes a broader view by showing that battery AI methods must be assessed across model quality, disturbance sensitivity, embedded feasibility, and system integration at the same time.
\item In this sense, the work is positioned between algorithm development and embedded systems engineering. It neither treats the battery estimator as a purely offline machine-learning problem nor reduces the hardware side to an implementation appendix.
\item This placement is especially important for battery-management applications, because the practical usefulness of an estimator depends on the full chain from measurement acquisition and signal quality to firmware integration and on-device inference behaviour.
\end{itemize}

\section{Technical Implications for AI-Based BMS}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The combined results imply that estimator selection for a BMS should begin with the target use case and the expected sensing conditions rather than with benchmark accuracy under ideal signals alone.
\item Robustness properties must be treated as a design criterion before and not after embedded compression, because pruning or quantization of a disturbance-sensitive model can further reduce its practical value.
\item The hardware chapter additionally shows that an adaptable research platform is not a secondary convenience, but an enabling condition for meaningful embedded-AI experimentation. The ability to exchange estimator methods, separate BMS logic from state-estimation logic, and validate new models in a controlled firmware environment is methodologically important.
\item For engineering practice, this means that AI-based battery estimation should be developed together with the execution environment, measurement chain, and integration path instead of being optimized only in an offline data-analysis setting.
\end{itemize}

\section{Integrated Design Guidelines}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Estimators should not be selected by clean-condition accuracy alone, because disturbance sensitivity and recovery behaviour strongly affect practical usefulness.
\item Robustness analysis should precede aggressive compression, since a highly compressed estimator with poor disturbance tolerance offers limited deployment value.
\item Quantization and pruning must be treated as architecture-dependent design choices rather than as universally transferable post-processing steps.
\item Dataset quality, estimator quality, and embedded execution quality should be analysed separately so that improvements in one domain are not mistaken for improvements in another.
\item Algorithmic claims gain practical relevance only when they are connected to a credible validation chain that includes sensing, firmware execution, and hardware integration.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Conclusion and Outlook}
\section{Overall Conclusion}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The work shows that deployment-oriented battery AI emerges only when model design, robustness, embedded execution, and hardware integration are treated as one linked problem.
\item Neural architectures enable powerful battery state estimation and ageing estimation, but their practical value depends on how they behave under disturbances and how efficiently they can be transferred to embedded hardware.
\item Robustness benchmarking reveals that estimator quality changes fundamentally once measurement corruption, timing irregularities, and initialization mismatch are considered explicitly.
\item Embedded implementation demonstrates that compression, latency, and memory demand are not secondary engineering details, but core evaluation dimensions for practical battery-management applications.
\item The combination of algorithmic studies and custom hardware integration therefore leads to a unified framework for analysing battery intelligence across performance, hardware, and deployment requirements.
\end{itemize}

\section{Boundary Conditions and Remaining Gaps}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The reported findings must be interpreted in the context of the available datasets, the selected estimator classes, and the concrete hardware environments used throughout the work.
\item The general design logic of the dissertation is transferable, but the exact quantitative trade-offs between accuracy, robustness, memory demand, and latency can shift when other cell chemistries, sensing chains, control architectures, or MCU families are considered.
\item Further validation across broader datasets, longer ageing horizons, and additional embedded targets is therefore necessary to consolidate the transferability of the derived design rules.
\end{itemize}

\section{Outlook}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Future work extends the embedded analysis by comparing pruning and quantization across a wider range of model architectures.
\item Further experiments on the BMS-STEP platform can deepen the relation between estimator design, signal quality, and practical system behaviour under laboratory and hardware-in-the-loop conditions.
\item Long-duration cycling and additional datasets can refine the calibration and validation of SoC and SoH methods across broader ageing states and operating conditions.
\item The longer-term objective is the transfer of deployment-oriented battery AI from controlled laboratory systems toward scalable and reproducible BMS integration in larger energy-storage applications.
\end{itemize}
