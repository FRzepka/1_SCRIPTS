Abstract
Robustness of battery State-of-Charge (SOC) estimation requires evidence beyond nominal accuracy. This study compares representatives of four major SOCestimation classes under one causal and cell-disjoint protocol: fixed-capacity
Coulomb counting (DM), SOH-adaptive Coulomb counting (HDM), a secondorder equivalent-circuit observer (HECM), and a recurrent data-driven estimator (DD). LFP aging trajectories span multiple load, thermal, and SOH conditions. The evaluation combines nominal accuracy against the dataset ground
truth, robustness under corrupted or unavailable measurements, recovery after
an initialization mismatch, and embedded execution cost. The six-cell macro
MAE under nominal inputs is 0.0690 for DM, 0.0412 for HDM, 0.0337 for
HECM, and 0.0258 for DD. DD also leads several disturbance cases, whereas
HECM provides the strongest observed recovery profile with a mean observed
time to persistent recovery or censoring of 1.20 h compared with 2.60 h for
DD. DM and HDM retain a cell-macro persistent-censor fraction of 0.89. The
changing order across disturbance families and score aggregations reveals complementary strengths instead of one consistently dominant implementation. On
the STM32 target, all isolated SOC cores reproduce their software references
and meet the one-second deadline, while direct measurements of latency, flash,

1. Introduction

43

1.1. Motivation and practical relevance

44

Reliable battery state estimation is a core BMS function because SOC and

45

SOH determine safety margins, usable energy, operating limits, charge con-

46

trol, and degradation-aware system management in both mobile and stationary
3

47

lithium-ion battery applications [1, 2, 3]. Practical deployment quality is not

48

defined by clean-condition accuracy alone, because real BMS operation is ex-

49

posed to sensor gain and offset errors, additive noise, initialization uncertainty

50

after restart, missing samples, irregular sampling, communication interruptions,

51

and transient signal spikes caused by measurement-chain limitations or distur-

52

bances in the surrounding system [4, 5, 6]. The relevant engineering question

53

is therefore not only whether an estimator achieves low MAE under nominal

54

measurements, but also whether it converges back to a reliable state after dis-

55

turbed initialization and whether it remains stable under physically plausible

56

measurement corruption.

57

As summarized conceptually in Figure 1, the present benchmark treats

58

deployment-facing estimator quality as a three-dimensional problem consisting

59

of nominal accuracy, recovery after state inconsistency, and robustness against

60

disturbed measurements and signal-integrity faults, embedded within broader

61

constraints on memory and execution time. The central question is how four

62

structurally different SOC-estimation classes, represented by the implementa-

63

tions studied here, respond to common failure mechanisms and microcontroller

64

constraints, and whether their nominal ranking remains informative under those

65

conditions. This distinction is essential because one implementation can be ac-

66

curate under clean measurements yet difficult to re-anchor, sensitive to a specific

67

input fault, or too costly under its trained inference semantics.

68

1.2. State of the art in battery state estimation

69

The literature commonly groups battery-state estimators into direct-measurement

70

or integration-based approaches, model-based approaches based on equivalent-

71

circuit or electrochemical models, hybrid approaches that combine physical

72

structure with learned components, and purely data-driven models based on

73

neural sequence learning [7, 1, 2, 3]. Direct-measurement approaches, typically

74

based on Coulomb counting, are attractive because they are simple, compu-

75

tationally inexpensive, and easy to deploy. However, they remain structurally

76

sensitive to current-gain error, missing data, and initialization errors because

77

they lack an internal correction mechanism [7, 4]. Model-based approaches,

78

especially ECM- and observer-based estimators, offer physical interpretability

79

and voltage-based correction capability, but their practical behavior depends

80

strongly on the quality of model parametrization, operating-point observability,

81

and measurement quality [8, 2, 4].

82

Hybrid estimators seek to combine physical state propagation with learned

83

adaptation of capacity, parameters, or auxiliary states, which has made joint

84

SOC/SOH estimation an active research direction [9, 10, 11]. This combination

85

can improve baseline calibration, while it does not necessarily eliminate struc-

86

tural sensitivity to measurement disturbances. Data-driven approaches based

87

on recurrent or sequence models can exploit nonlinear cross-relations between

88

current, voltage, temperature, and derived online features, and recent work has

89

also demonstrated their embedded feasibility through TinyML-oriented imple5

mentations, pruning, and quantization [12, 13, 14, 15, 3].

1.3. Performance and embedded deployment gap

92

A large share of the SOC estimation literature still emphasizes clean-data ac-

93

curacy, average error metrics, or computational efficiency, while comparatively

94

fewer studies analyze how estimators behave under realistic measurement faults

95

and timing corruption [1, 13, 14, 15]. Existing robustness studies often remain

96

confined to a single model family, for example observer-based robustness against

97

modeling and measurement errors [4] or robust estimation concepts for specific

98

BMS architectures under noise and uncertainty [5], whereas cross-family com-

99

parisons under the same disturbance protocol and under comparable embedded

100

constraints remain limited. Benchmarking studies in the broader BMS con-

101

text underline the importance of reproducible scenario design and deployment-

102

relevant validation, but they do not by themselves close the gap between clean-

103

data estimator comparison and measurement-level performance benchmarking

104

across fundamentally different estimator classes [6]. This gap is especially rele-

105

vant in embedded BMS design, where estimator selection must jointly consider

106

nominal accuracy, convergence, robustness under disturbed measurements, and

107

computational constraints.

108

1.4. Contribution and study objectives

109

This work addresses the gap through three linked questions: whether clean-

110

data accuracy predicts performance under measurement and signal-integrity dis-

111

turbances, which structural mechanisms govern degradation and recovery, and

112

how the resulting behavior trades against embedded execution cost. To an-

113

swer them, four deployment-oriented representatives are executed under one

114

causal protocol on cell-disjoint LFP aging data covering multiple load and SOH

115

states. The campaign combines physically interpretable measurement, timing,

116

availability, quantization, and initialization disturbances with a common recov-

117

ery criterion, cell-level uncertainty analysis, a paired SOH-input ablation, and

118

STM32 measurements of the isolated SOC cores.

2.2.4. Data-driven SOC model (DD)

187

The data-driven SOC model is a GRU-based recurrent estimator with the

188

feature vector {U [V], I [A], T [◦ C], SOH, Qc , dU/dt, dI/dt, ∆t}. The bench-

189

marked, structurally pruned and fine-tuned model consists of a single-layer GRU

190

with hidden size 67 followed by an MLP head with one hidden layer of size 96

191

and a sigmoid output layer. During optimization, the recurrent core is exposed

192

to causal sequence segments of length LSOC = 2024 samples. Within this fea-

193

ture set, Qc is exactly the same online cumulative-charge state defined in (1).

194

It is included explicitly so that the neural estimator receives the same charge-

195

throughput information that also drives DM and HDM. The derivative channels

196

are computed online as the finite differences dI/dt(k) = (Ik − Ik−1 )/∆tk and

197

dU/dt(k) = (Uk − Uk−1 )/∆tk . They provide local transient information that is

198

not captured by the absolute channels alone. The additional feature ∆tk allows

199

the network to distinguish amplitude changes from sampling-interval changes

200

under irregular timing. During the primary benchmark, each output is calcu-

201

lated from the latest causal 2024-sample window and recurrent state is reset

202

between windows, matching training and validation. This configuration uses
10

203

measured and causally reconstructed inputs up to the current sample. The

204

hardware analysis additionally evaluates continuous hidden-state forwarding as

205

a deployment variant and reports its accuracy and resource demand separately.

2.3. SOH estimation method

207

The shared SOH estimator is an LSTM-based sequence-to-sequence model

208

that operates on hourly aggregated online features derived from {U [V], I [A],

209

T [◦ C], EFC, Qc }, where the aggregation uses the statistics mean, standard

210

deviation, minimum, and maximum for each feature [16, 17, 18, 19]. This

211

transforms the five base channels into a 20-dimensional hourly feature vector.

212

Architecturally, the SOH network first projects the aggregated input through

213

a two-layer feature embedding block with size 128, layer normalization, GELU

214

activations, and dropout, then processes the sequence with a two-layer unidirec-

215

tional LSTM of hidden size 112, and finally maps the recurrent output through

216

three residual MLP blocks and a prediction head with hidden size 160 to obtain

217

the SOH estimate for each hourly step.

218

During optimization, the SOH estimator is exposed to causal hourly se-

219

quence segments, and benchmark execution processes one hourly aggregate at a

220

time with causal hidden- and cell-state forwarding. This allows the estimator to

221

accumulate degradation context without access to future information. The first

222

hourly output is anchored with the configured initial SOH value before subse-

223

quent hourly predictions are taken directly from the recurrent model output.

224

The SOH prediction is updated at hourly cadence and held piecewise constant

225

between update points. This online update structure matches the gradually

226

evolving capacity fade in the dataset and supplies the latest SOH context to

227

the faster SOC estimators. In the HDM, the predicted SOH enters the SOC

228

2.4. Model and target-hardware preparation

235

The software benchmark uses trained floating-point model parameters and

236

input scalers fixed before holdout evaluation. Neural training, validation, hyper-

237

parameter selection, scaler fitting, pruning, model selection, and HECM param-

238

eter identification use the fixed training and validation split. The HPPC-derived

239

HECM lookup surfaces were identified from this model-development subset and

240

fixed before benchmark evaluation. The cells C09, C13, C15, C25, C27, and

241

C29 are reserved for holdout evaluation. The compact SOC-GRU has hidden

242

size 67 and the shared SOH-LSTM has hidden size 112. The continuous C29

243

lifecycle replay is identified separately as a mechanism-focused diagnostic.

244

The target-hardware experiment evaluates the isolated SOC cores on a NUCLEO-

245

H753ZI board with a 480 MHz STM32H753 Cortex-M7. To separate SOC imple-

246

mentation cost from the shared SOH service, the board receives the precomputed

247

causal SOH trace used by the software reference. The SOH-LSTM itself is not

248

executed on the microcontroller. Numerical equivalence and inference latency

249

are measured directly on the board. Execution time is recorded for every valid

250

inference with the processor-integrated hardware cycle counter, and each cell

251

sequence is replayed three times. Flash occupancy is calculated from the com-

252

piled firmware regions that must be stored in the microcontroller’s non-volatile

253

program memory.

254

Runtime RAM is divided into statically allocated variable storage, dynam-

255

ically allocated memory, and temporary memory used during nested function

256

calls. The static component includes both initialized variables and variables

257

set to zero during system startup. Maximum temporary function-call mem-

258

ory is measured by filling the available stack region with a known pattern be-

259

fore initialization. After a representative 2025-sample replay of cell C09, the

260

deepest overwritten address identifies the maximum function-call memory used.

261

Dynamic-memory management is instrumented to record the maximum cumu-

262

lative allocation reached during execution. The reported peak RAM combines
12

263

these three contributions and covers startup, protocol handling, and estimator

264

execution. An unused compilation-time memory reserve is kept separate from

265

the measured demand. The exact rolling-window DD produces two valid calls

266

after its 2023-sample warm-up in this memory replay, so its measured stack

267

maximum applies to those calls, while its dominant static window allocation

268

is determined at link time. Predictions are compared with both the software

269

implementation and dataset SOC. These measurements quantify the resource

270

demand of the isolated SOC implementations with SOH supplied as an external

271

causal trace.

2.5. Robustness benchmark design

273

The measurement-disturbance branch manipulates current, voltage, tem-

274

perature, sample availability, and sampling time, all of which are measured

275

or reconstructed online by a BMS. A separate initialization branch acts on

276

deployment-accessible estimator states. This structure concentrates the bench-

277

mark on reproducible disturbances in the sensing and signal-processing chain

278

[6]. Figure 3 groups the scenarios into input disturbances, initialization errors,

279

and signal-integrity faults, while Table 2 defines their implementation.

280

The disturbance magnitudes follow three practical levels of justification:

281

deployment-level sensing limits, adverse measurement corruption, and signal-

282

integrity stress conditions [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Input disturbances include

283

gain error, sensor noise, voltage spikes, and ADC quantization. The remaining

284

scenarios address estimator initialization, sample availability, and sampling-time

285

integrity. Together they form a controlled and reproducible benchmark matrix

286

for exposing estimator-specific failure mechanisms under a common protocol.

2.6. Metrics and evaluation criteria

288

For deployment-oriented interpretation, the benchmark results are synthe-

289

sized along three performance dimensions: nominal accuracy under baseline con-

290

ditions, recovery after initialization mismatch, and robustness under sustained

291

or transient measurement corruption and signal unavailability. Disturbance-

292

related global performance is quantified through MAE, RMSE, bias, maximum
15

293

error, and the 95th percentile absolute error, because these metrics provide a

294

compact view of average performance, systematic deviation, and tail behavior

295

across the selected evaluation windows. Local disturbance performance is an-

296

alyzed through scenario-specific metrics such as jump counts, output variance,

297

absolute-error variance, and excess rolling MAE, because several disturbances do

298

not primarily manifest as window-wide MAE collapse but as short-term output

299

fluctuations, delayed drift, or slow re-synchronization. Whenever a disturbance

300

mask exists, the analysis further separates pre-disturbance, disturbed, and post-

301

disturbance behavior in order to quantify disturbance-window error growth and

302

residual post-disturbance error.

303

ADC quantization, current-gain error, voltage and temperature disturbances,

304

missing samples, and irregular sampling are interpreted primarily through global

305

metrics. Burst dropout, voltage spikes, current-noise detail, and initialization

306

mismatch additionally use dedicated local evidence because their most relevant

307

effects are episodic. The canonical initialization-recovery analysis compares each

308

perturbed estimator trajectory with its cell-, window-, model-, SOH-, and seed-

309

matched correctly initialized trajectory. The resulting paired trajectory differ-

310

ence is the recovery signal, while absolute error against dataset SOC remains

311

the accuracy measure. A first qualifying entry occurs when the paired differ-

312

ence enters a 0.02 SOC band for at least 300 s. Persistent recovery is the first

313

subsequent point after which the difference remains within this band for the

314

rest of the 24-h observation horizon. Runs without this endpoint are right-

315

censored. First-entry time, later departure from the band, censored and relapse

316

fractions, and excess-error area under the curve provide complementary recov-

317

ery diagnostics. The protocol maps a −0.10 SOC-equivalent mismatch into the

318

5. Discussion

650

5.1. Structural interpretation of the representatives

651

The four implementations expose distinct relationships between their update

652

mechanisms and observed disturbance responses. DM has the largest nominal

653

error and reacts strongly to persistent gain error and missing samples because

654

its current-integration structure lacks voltage-residual correction. Its practi-

655

cal full-charge anchor limits accumulated drift, while the lifecycle experiment

656

shows that the reset restructures the gain-error contribution without removing

657

it uniformly. HDM adapts the capacity denominator through learned SOH and

658

thereby reduces capacity mismatch, yet it retains the same charge-integration

659

core. Its similar response to current-gain error, missing charge information, and

660

ADC quantization follows directly from this shared structure.

661

HECM combines current-driven state propagation with a voltage residual

662

and has the strongest observed recovery profile under the tested initialization

663

mapping. The common observation starts after 0.562 h and resolves subse-

664

quent re-anchoring behavior. High current noise and burst dropout produce

665

the largest HECM point penalties because corrupted or frozen inputs affect

666

both state propagation and observer correction. The confidence interval for

667

high current noise includes zero and shows substantial between-cell uncertainty.

668

The fixed HPPC-derived parameter surfaces provide the calibrated model basis

669

for this comparison. Their table-dependent flash demand and voltage-feedback

670

correction form the central deployment trade-off of HECM.

671

DD has the lowest nominal cell-macro error and the best adverse current-

672

gain score in this comparison. It is also comparatively resilient to isolated

673

sample loss. Event-aligned voltage spikes expose a recurrent transient that full-

674

window MAE hides. The selected voltage and derivative features and the rolling

675

recurrent context provide a plausible transmission path for this response and

676

identify a concrete target for feature ablation and disturbance-aware training.

37

677

5.2. Why nominal accuracy is insufficient

678

Nominal MAE describes average agreement with the dataset ground truth

679

under clean inputs. The disturbance and recovery experiments add the DD

680

voltage-spike transient, HECM re-anchoring behavior, shared-SOH feature cou-

681

pling, and model-specific responses to gain error and missing information. Em-

682

bedded measurements add latency, memory, and inference-state semantics. These

683

dimensions create a deployment profile for each implementation, while the weight-

684

ing analysis shows how application priorities reshape their combined scores.

685

Estimator selection can therefore begin with the relevant fault and recovery

686

requirements and then incorporate the available compute, memory, and state-

687

service architecture.

688

5.3. Implications for BMS deployment

689

Among the tested representatives, DD has the lowest nominal error against

690

the dataset ground truth and remains a strong candidate when its recurrent

691

implementation cost and voltage-spike response fit the application. HECM is

692

preferable when persistent correction after a state mismatch has high priority

693

and a larger flash image is available. DM and HDM remain attractive when

694

deterministic sub-microsecond execution and small memory dominate. Their

695

integration structure places correspondingly greater requirements on current

696

calibration, reliable sampling, and full-charge anchoring. These mechanism-

697

based trade-offs connect the benchmark results directly to BMS deployment

698

priorities.

699

The STM32 results connect algorithmic behavior with deployment cost. All

700

four isolated SOC cores meet the one-second deadline in the tested replay, while

701

their timing margins span more than six orders of magnitude between DM/HDM

702

and exact rolling-window DD. Continuous-state DD removes most of the rolling-

703

window cost by forwarding its recurrent state. The firmware measurements

704

include flash, maximum call-stack use, dynamic-memory demand, and numerical

705

equivalence. Integration of the shared SOH-LSTM, energy measurement, pack

706

communication, and concurrent BMS scheduling forms the next system-level

5.4. Limitations and future extensions

718

The conclusions are grounded in four specified implementations and six hold-

719

out cells from one controlled LFP aging dataset. Alternative reset logic, ECM

720

structures, neural architectures, features, and training data can produce differ-

39

721

ent estimator profiles. The low- and middle-load strata contain multiple cells,

722

whereas C29 supplies one exploratory high-load trajectory and C27 contributes

723

no mid-life or aged window. The reported confidence intervals therefore quan-

724

tify the available between-cell variation within this design. Transfer to other

725

chemistries, cell formats, pack configurations, thermal environments, and field

726

duty cycles requires corresponding cell-disjoint validation. Nominal accuracy is

727

referenced to one consistently reconstructed offline SOC ground truth based on

728

Coulomb counting and voltage anchors.

729

The disturbance campaign concentrates on the BMS measurement chain,

730

signal availability and timing, and deployment-accessible initialization states.

731

Model-parameter mismatch, pack-level interactions, and supervisory BMS faults

732

form a separate validation layer. The initialization experiment maps an SOC-

733

equivalent offset into each estimator’s available state and observes recovery from

734

the common 0.562-h boundary. The resulting times characterize operational re-

735

anchoring under these class-specific interventions. The continuous C29 replay

736

complements the six-cell windows with a mechanism-focused lifecycle view. The

737

hourly SOH service represents gradual capacity fade, and channel-removal ex-

738

periments would further isolate the DD voltage-spike transmission path. HECM

739

uses HPPC-derived parameter surfaces identified from the development cells and

740

held fixed throughout evaluation, so the present results characterize measure-

741

ment robustness around this calibrated model.

742

The hardware profile covers the isolated SOC firmware with SOH supplied

743

as an external causal trace. Runtime-RAM watermarks come from the repre-

744

sentative C09 replay, and the rolling DD stack maximum is based on its valid

745

post-warm-up calls. The next hardware stage combines the SOC and SOH

746

services, measures energy demand, and evaluates estimator scheduling along-

747

side communication and supervisory BMS tasks. Broader data campaigns can

748

then extend the disturbance protocol to interacting faults, additional operating

749

environments, and pack-level closed-loop behavior.

40

750

6. Conclusion

751

This study establishes a common robustness and embedded-performance

752

benchmark for four structurally different SOC-estimation implementations. DD

753

achieves the lowest nominal cell-macro error and the strongest results across sev-

754

eral disturbance families, while local and hardware-specific analyses reveal its

755

voltage-spike transient and rolling-window cost. HECM provides the strongest

756

observed recovery profile and voltage-residual correction, together with the

757

largest flash demand and pronounced responses to current noise and prolonged

758

dropout. DM and HDM provide deterministic sub-microsecond execution with

759

minimal memory, while their current-integration structure increases sensitivity

760

to gain error, missing charge information, and physical re-anchoring.

761

The combined evidence turns estimator selection into a deployment-oriented

762

comparison of accuracy, disturbance-specific robustness, recovery, latency, flash,

763

and runtime RAM. The STM32 measurements confirm numerical equivalence

764

with the software implementations and one-second execution of all isolated SOC

765

cores, while the DD deployment variants show how recurrent-state handling

766

changes both cost and transient behavior. Scenario-level evidence remains cen-

767

tral because the preferred implementation changes with the sensing fault profile,

768

recovery requirement, and available hardware margin. This framework provides

769

a direct basis for extending validation to complete SOC–SOH execution, concur-

770

rent BMS tasks, additional operating environments, and pack-level applications.
